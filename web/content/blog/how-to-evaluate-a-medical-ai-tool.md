To evaluate a medical AI tool before you adopt it, run it on questions your team already knows the answers to, open every source it cites, and see what it does when the evidence runs out. A vendor's demo shows how the tool handles the vendor's examples, and your own test shows how it handles your work.

## Why isn't the vendor's demo enough?

A demo is chosen to show a product at its best. The questions are ones the tool handles well, the answers have been seen before, and nobody in the room is checking references against PubMed as the answers appear.

Your team's questions are different. They include the rare condition, the drug interaction your pharmacists keep getting asked about, and the question where the honest answer is that the evidence is thin. Those are the cases that decide whether a tool helps or hurts.

## How do you build a test set from your own questions?

Write the questions and the expected answers before you run anything. Recording the answer first stops you from grading generously after you have seen the output.

A useful test set mixes:

- Routine questions with clear, textbook answers, such as whether angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors can cause a dry cough.
- Hard questions that need specific evidence, where a vague answer would be easy to give.
- Questions with thin evidence, where the right response is to say so.
- Questions with a false premise, to see whether the tool corrects the premise or plays along.
- Questions phrased the way your staff or patients actually phrase them, typos and all.

For each, note the answer you expect and, where you have them, the sources you would cite. Have a clinician or subject expert approve the answer key.

## How do you check the sources a tool cites?

Open every one. For each reference, confirm that the PubMed ID (PMID) or digital object identifier (DOI) resolves to a real record, that the title, authors, journal and year match, and that the abstract supports the specific sentence it is attached to. The procedure is laid out in [how to verify an AI-generated reference list](/blog/verify-ai-generated-reference-list).

Expect two kinds of failure. Some references will not exist. Others will be real papers that say something different from the claim, which is harder to spot and covered in [why a real citation can still be the wrong citation](/blog/real-citation-wrong-claim).

Published research shows how large both problems can be. Walters and Wilder, writing in Scientific Reports in 2023, examined 636 citations across 84 literature reviews generated by an earlier version and a newer version of a widely used chatbot, on 42 topics from many fields, not only medicine. Fabricated citations made up 55 percent of the earlier version's citations and 18 percent of the newer version's. Among the citations that were real, 43 percent of the earlier version's and 24 percent of the newer version's contained substantive errors.

## Does the tool say when it does not know?

A medical tool that always produces a confident answer will sometimes produce a confident wrong one. Look at what happens on your thin-evidence and false-premise questions. A good tool says what is supported, says what is not, and does not paper over the gap with a citation.

The design case for this is made in [why medical AI should be allowed to say "not supported"](/blog/why-medical-ai-should-say-not-supported). When you score a tool, count an honest "not supported" as a correct response when the evidence really is missing.

## Is the tool consistent?

Ask the same question several times, then ask it again in different words. Compare the claims and the sources across runs.

Some variation in wording is normal. **Variation in substance is a warning sign:** a claim that appears in one run and is contradicted in the next, or a reference list that changes completely each time. If the answer depends on how a question is phrased, your staff will get different answers to the same problem.

## What failure modes should you look for?

Keep a list and tally each one as you test:

- References that do not exist
- Real references that do not support the claim
- Claims about one population drawn from a study of another
- Preliminary findings stated as settled
- Caveats from the source dropped from the answer
- Outdated evidence presented as current
- A false premise accepted without comment
- Confidence that does not match the evidence

The pattern of failures tells you more than an overall score. A tool that sometimes misses but always flags its uncertainty is easier to work with than one that is usually right and never says when it might be wrong.

## How should you record what you find?

Judge each answer claim by claim instead of as a whole. Mark each claim supported, partly supported, not supported, or backed by a source that does not exist. Note whether the tool flagged the problem itself or left it for you to find.

Keep the raw outputs, the date and the version of the tool you tested. If you can, have reviewers grade outputs without knowing which tool produced them, so their expectations do not color the scores.

## When should you test again?

Test again whenever the tool changes. The Walters and Wilder results show that two versions of the same chatbot can behave very differently on citations, so a test of last year's version tells you little about this year's. Ask vendors to tell you when the underlying model changes, and rerun your test set when it does. For more to ask them, see [questions to ask a medical AI vendor about citations](/blog/questions-to-ask-a-medical-ai-vendor-about-citations).

You can practice this kind of testing on Luma's [free demo](/demo): paste an answer to a question you know well and see which claims it links to sources and which it flags.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the most important thing to test in a medical AI tool?

Test whether its claims are supported by the sources it cites. An answer can read well and still rest on references that do not exist or do not say what the answer claims.

### How many test questions do you need?

Enough to cover the kinds of questions your team actually asks, including the hard and uncertain ones. A small set you check carefully, claim by claim, tells you more than a large set you skim.

### Should clinicians be involved in evaluating a medical AI tool?

Yes. Clinicians or subject experts should write or approve the expected answers and judge whether the tool's claims are right for the audience, because a source check alone cannot tell you whether an answer makes clinical sense.

### How can you tell whether a tool makes up references?

Open every reference it gives and confirm it in PubMed or through its digital object identifier. A reference that cannot be found, or whose details do not match the record, should be treated as fabricated until shown otherwise.
