AI chat tools can be useful for understanding medical words, organizing your thoughts and preparing questions before an appointment. They can also be confidently wrong, so treat what they say as a starting point to check and discuss with your clinician, never as a diagnosis or a treatment plan.

Here is what to know before you type your first health question.

## What can AI help you with?

Used carefully, an AI tool can save you time in a few specific ways.

- Explaining terms: if a letter or a visit summary mentions "hypertension," an AI tool can tell you it means high blood pressure and put the explanation in plain language.
- Preparing questions: you can describe what is on your mind and have the tool help you turn scattered worries into a short, clear list for your clinician.
- Organizing information: it can help you arrange symptoms, dates and the names of your medicines into a tidy list to bring to an appointment.
- Background reading: a general explanation of a condition or a test can make a conversation with your care team easier to follow.

What these uses have in common is that they help you take part in your own care. None of them asks the tool to decide anything for you.

## What can AI get wrong?

AI tools write fluent, confident text whether or not the content is correct. A wrong answer usually reads exactly like a right one, and some mistakes are hard to spot for that reason.

Problems to watch for include:

- Stating something as settled when the evidence is mixed or still early.
- Mixing up drug names, conditions or tests that sound alike.
- Giving general information that does not fit your age, your history or the other medicines you take.
- Citing sources that do not exist, or that say something different from what the answer claims.

The last one can be hard to believe. A citation with authors, a journal and a year can be made up entirely, because AI tools generate text that looks like a reference without looking anything up. We explain how that happens in [what a fabricated citation is](/blog/what-is-a-fabricated-citation).

**An AI tool does not know you.** It has not examined you, it does not have your records unless you share them, and it cannot weigh your situation the way your care team can.

## How can you check what an AI tool tells you?

You do not need medical training to do a basic check.

1. Ask the tool where the information comes from. If it names a study or an organization, look for that source yourself.
2. For research papers, search the title on PubMed, the free database of biomedical research maintained by the National Library of Medicine. If you cannot find the paper, be cautious about the claim attached to it.
3. Compare the answer with patient information from your clinic, your hospital or your pharmacist.
4. Notice how certain the answer sounds. Words like "always," "never" and "guaranteed" deserve extra caution in health information.
5. Write down anything you are unsure about and ask your clinician.

Our guide on [how to check if a PubMed citation is real](/blog/how-to-check-if-a-pubmed-citation-is-real) walks through step 2 in more detail.

## What should you never use AI for?

Some decisions belong with your clinician, and an AI answer should never stand in for them.

- Do not start, stop or change a medicine, or how much of it you take, based on an AI answer.
- Do not rely on an AI tool to decide whether a symptom is an emergency. If you think you may be having an emergency, call your local emergency number.
- Do not use an AI answer to interpret your own test results. Ask the clinician who ordered the test what the result means for you.
- Do not treat an AI answer as a second opinion. A second opinion comes from a qualified clinician who can review your case.

## Why do AI answers sound so sure?

AI chat tools are built to produce smooth, complete-sounding text. They do not pause the way a person might when they are unsure, and they will not always say "I don't know" when they should. Polished writing can make a weak answer feel trustworthy.

A useful habit is to read every AI health answer as if a stranger had said it to you. It might be right. It still needs checking before you act on it or repeat it.

## How do you bring AI research to an appointment?

It is reasonable to tell your clinician what you have read, including where you read it. A little preparation makes that conversation quicker and more useful.

- Bring a short list of questions rather than a long printout.
- Say that the information came from an AI tool, so your clinician knows how to weigh it.
- Ask directly: "I read this. Does it apply to me?"
- Ask which sources your clinician trusts for further reading.
- Write down the answers, or ask for a written summary of what was discussed.

Our article on [preparing for a doctor's visit](/blog/prepare-for-doctors-visit-what-ai-can-do) has a fuller checklist, and [how to keep track of what your doctor said](/blog/how-to-keep-track-of-what-your-doctor-said) covers what to do after the appointment.

## What about privacy?

Before you type in personal details, check the tool's privacy terms and think about what you want to share. Many general questions can be asked without your name, your date of birth or other details that identify you.

If a question only makes sense with your personal history attached, it is probably a question for your clinician.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it safe to ask AI about my health?

It can be a helpful way to learn terms and prepare questions, as long as you treat the answers as information to check rather than advice. Decisions about diagnosis, medicines and treatment belong with your clinician.

### Can AI diagnose me?

No. An AI tool has not examined you and does not know your full history, so use it to prepare questions and bring any concerns to your clinician.

### How do I know if an AI health answer is accurate?

Look for the sources behind it, check that they exist and say what the answer claims, and compare the information with what your care team gives you. When in doubt, ask your clinician.

### Should I tell my doctor I used AI?

Yes, it helps to say where your information came from. Your clinician can then explain what applies to you and what does not.
