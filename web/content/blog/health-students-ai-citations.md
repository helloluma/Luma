Students in health programs should know that AI tools can produce citations to papers that do not exist, and real citations that do not support the sentence they are attached to. Use AI to study and to plan searches, but cite only sources you have found and read yourself, and follow your program's policy on AI use.

## How often do AI tools invent citations?

Often enough that any AI-generated reference should be treated as unverified until you check it.

A 2023 study examined 636 citations across 84 literature reviews on 42 topics, written by an earlier version and a newer version of a widely used chatbot. The study covered many fields, not only medicine. It found that 55 percent of the citations from the earlier version and 18 percent from the newer version were fabricated. Among the citations that were real, 43 percent of the earlier version's and 24 percent of the newer version's contained substantive errors (Walters and Wilder, Scientific Reports).

For biomedical work specifically, an audit published in The Lancet in 2026 reports that prior studies estimate 30 to 69 percent of references generated by large language models in biomedical contexts are fabricated.

The same audit looked at published papers, and the problem was there too. It found 4,046 fabricated references across 2,810 biomedical papers. A reference list in a published article is not proof that every entry is real.

## Why do AI tools make up references?

A large language model writes by predicting likely text from patterns it learned. A reference has a familiar shape (authors, title, journal, year, volume, pages), and a model can produce that shape fluently without checking whether the paper exists. Unless the tool is built to search a database and confirm each source, nothing stops it from citing a paper that was never written. There is a fuller explanation in [what a fabricated citation is and why AI tools produce them](/blog/what-is-a-fabricated-citation).

## What counts as an academic integrity problem?

Programs set their own rules, so read your syllabus and your school's policy on AI before you use it for coursework. Whatever the policy says, these habits keep you on safe ground:

- **Cite only what you have read.** Listing a source you have not opened misrepresents your work, even when the source is real.
- **Own your reference list.** A fabricated reference in your paper is your error. Saying an AI tool produced it does not change who submitted it.
- **Disclose AI use the way your program asks.** Some instructors want a note on how you used it. Others do not allow it for certain assignments.
- **Write in your own words.** Do not paste AI-written text into an assignment as your own unless your instructor allows it.

## How should you check a citation an AI tool gives you?

Treat every AI-provided reference as a lead to follow, then check it:

1. Search the title in PubMed. If the citation includes a PubMed ID (PMID), look that up too and make sure both lead to the same record.
2. Compare the authors, journal, year, volume and pages with the record.
3. Read the abstract, and the full text when you can, and confirm the paper supports the exact sentence you want to cite.
4. If you cannot find the paper, do not cite it. Find a real source for the claim or drop the claim.

Step-by-step guidance is in [how to check if a PubMed citation is real](/blog/how-to-check-if-a-pubmed-citation-is-real), and [how to read a PubMed abstract in five minutes](/blog/how-to-read-a-pubmed-abstract) helps with step three.

## Where can AI help you study?

Used as a study aid, AI can save time without putting your integrity at risk.

- **Explaining concepts.** Ask for a plain explanation of a mechanism or a term, then confirm it in your textbook or lecture notes.
- **Quizzing you.** Ask for practice questions on material you have already studied, and check the answers against your course sources.
- **Planning searches.** Ask for synonyms and related terms, then turn them into a PubMed search. Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) can make that search more precise, as shown in [how to use MeSH terms to search PubMed](/blog/how-to-use-mesh-terms).
- **Outlining.** Use it to organize your own notes into a structure, then fill that structure with sources you found yourself.
- **Editing your writing,** where your program allows it.

In each case, AI helps you think and organize. Your evidence comes from the literature.

## What habits protect you across a whole program?

- **Keep a source log.** For each source, record the PMID or digital object identifier (DOI), where you found it, and the sentence it supports. You will thank yourself when a reviewer or instructor asks.
- **Import citations from real records.** Bringing references into a citation manager from PubMed, instead of typing them, cuts down on copying errors.
- **Check references you borrow from published papers.** Following a paper's reference list is a good way to find sources, but published lists can contain errors and, as the audit above found, fabricated entries.
- **Ask your librarians.** Health sciences librarians teach literature searching and can help when you cannot find a source or are unsure whether one is real.
- **Be suspicious of a perfect source.** A reference that says exactly what you need, in exactly the words you hoped for, deserves a closer look before it goes into your paper.

These habits carry into practice. Clinicians, researchers and writers all depend on being able to trace a statement back to the evidence behind it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I cite a source that an AI tool recommended?
Only after you have found the source yourself, confirmed its details against a database record such as PubMed, and read enough of it to know it supports your sentence. If you cannot find it, do not cite it.

### Why do AI tools make up citations?
A language model writing from memory produces text shaped like a reference based on patterns, and it does not check whether the paper exists unless the tool is built to search a database. The result can be a convincing citation to a paper that was never published.

### Is using AI for studying against academic integrity rules?
It depends on your program's policy, so check your syllabus and your school's rules before using it for coursework. Citing sources you have not read, or sources that do not exist, is a problem whatever tool produced them.

### Are references in published papers always accurate?
No. An audit published in The Lancet in 2026 found 4,046 fabricated references across 2,810 biomedical papers, so check any reference you copy from another paper's list.
