Evidence strength is how much confidence the available research justifies in a specific claim. It depends on how the studies were designed, how large they were, whether they agree with one another and how directly they address the claim, so finding one real paper that supports a sentence answers only part of the question.

When you check a claim, you are asking two things. First, does a source say what the claim says? Second, how much weight can that source bear? The first is support. The second is strength.

## How is evidence strength different from evidence support?

Support is a yes or no question about one source and one claim: does this paper report what the sentence says? Strength is a judgment about how far the evidence can be trusted, usually across several sources.

The two can come apart. **A claim can be fully supported by weak evidence**, such as a single small study or a laboratory experiment. A claim can also have strong evidence behind it and still be mis-cited, if the paper attached to it is the wrong one. Our post on [why a real citation can still be the wrong citation](/blog/real-citation-wrong-claim) covers that second failure.

Treating support and strength as one thing leads to a familiar mistake. A sentence states a preliminary finding as settled fact, the citation is real, and the check passes because the paper does say something like it.

## What makes evidence stronger or weaker?

Five factors raise or lower strength. Formal grading systems used by guideline panels weigh many of the same ones.

1. Study design. Some designs answer cause-and-effect questions better than others. A randomized controlled trial assigns people to groups by chance, which helps separate the effect of a treatment from everything else that differs between people. Observational studies can show that two things go together, but they have a harder time showing that one causes the other, and case reports, expert opinion, and laboratory and animal work sit lower still for questions about effects in people. The [hierarchy of evidence](/blog/hierarchy-of-evidence) lays out the usual order. Design is only a starting point, though: a small, poorly run trial can tell you less than a large, careful observational study.
2. Size and precision. Small studies are more easily swayed by chance. A larger study, or several studies pooled in a meta-analysis, gives a more precise estimate of an effect, and a precise estimate is harder to dismiss as a fluke.
3. Consistency. Evidence is stronger when several studies, run by different groups in different settings, point the same way. One striking result that nobody has repeated is weaker than a pattern seen again and again, which is why [one study is rarely the final word](/blog/why-one-study-is-rarely-the-final-word).
4. Directness. Direct evidence studies the same people, the same intervention and the same outcome the claim is about. Evidence becomes indirect when the study was done in a different population, such as adults when the claim is about children, or in cells or animals, or when it measured a lab marker instead of a health outcome people would notice. A drug that lowers a number on a blood test has not, by that fact alone, been shown to prevent the illness the number is linked to. Our post on [why animal and lab studies do not prove effects in people](/blog/why-animal-and-lab-studies-do-not-prove-effects-in-people) goes further.
5. Risk of bias. Some studies guard against systematic error better than others. Keeping group assignments concealed, blinding participants and assessors, following up everyone who enrolled and choosing outcomes in advance all make a result more trustworthy, and gaps in any of these weaken it.

## How does the claim itself change the strength you need?

The more specific the claim, the more specific the evidence it needs. Take a textbook example: angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, a common class of blood pressure medicine, can cause a dry cough. That is a well-established side effect, and a general statement that it can happen is easy to support.

A claim about how often the cough happens, or in whom, is a different claim. It needs evidence that measured frequency in the relevant group. A source that only says the cough is a known side effect supports the first sentence and not the second.

Wording works the same way. "May reduce", "is associated with" and "reduces" are three different claims. Each asks for a different level of evidence, and the verb should match what the studies can actually show.

## How should writers handle evidence strength?

- Name the kind of study when it matters: "in a small trial", "in a study of mice", "in a review of several trials".
- Match the verb to the design. Observational findings are associations, and early findings are preliminary.
- Keep the hedges the authors used. If the paper says "may", your sentence should too.
- When strong and weak evidence point in different directions, say so instead of choosing the source that fits the sentence.

## How should builders show evidence strength?

A verification tool should keep support and strength apart, in the data and on the screen. A claim marked "supported" with a single case report behind it should not look the same as a claim backed by several large trials.

- Store the study type for each source alongside the support judgment.
- Show the source next to the claim, so a reader can judge design, size and directness for themselves.
- If you show a single score, keep the factors behind it available, so a reader can see which one is weak.
- Say plainly when the system worked from abstracts alone, since an abstract may leave out details of design and size.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does evidence strength mean?

Evidence strength is how much confidence the available research justifies in a claim. It depends on study design, size, consistency across studies, directness and risk of bias, not only on whether a supporting paper exists.

### Can a claim be supported but still weak?

Yes. A claim can match what a source says while the source is a single small study, an animal experiment or a lab finding, which supports the claim but cannot bear much weight.

### Is a randomized controlled trial always strong evidence?

No. Randomized controlled trials are good at showing cause and effect, but a small, poorly run or indirect trial can still provide weak evidence for a particular claim.

### What is indirect evidence?

Indirect evidence comes from studies that differ from the claim in population, intervention or outcome, such as research in animals, in a different age group, or on a lab marker instead of a health outcome. It can inform a claim but supports it less firmly than direct evidence.
