PubMed is a free search engine for biomedical and life sciences literature. It is maintained by the National Library of Medicine, which is part of the National Institutes of Health in the United States.

Anyone can search it without an account at [pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), and it is a standard starting point for finding published medical research.

## What does PubMed contain?

PubMed is mostly a database of citations and abstracts. A typical record includes:

- The article title, the authors and their affiliations
- The journal, year, volume, issue and pages
- The abstract, when the journal provides one
- A PubMed ID (PMID), the unique number assigned to each record
- Often a digital object identifier (DOI), which points to the article on the publisher's site
- Links to the full text, where it is available
- For many records, Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), the standard subject terms that describe what the article is about

What PubMed usually does not hold is the full article itself. The full text lives with the publisher or, for many articles, in PubMed Central, a separate free archive of full-text articles that the National Library of Medicine also runs. When a free full-text version exists, the PubMed record links to it. Our post on [how PubMed Central differs from PubMed](/blog/pubmed-central-vs-pubmed) covers the two side by side.

## Who maintains PubMed, and why does that matter?

The [National Library of Medicine](https://www.nlm.nih.gov/) builds and runs PubMed as a public service. Two things follow from that.

First, it is free to search, so anyone can check the same record a researcher, a clinician or a journalist checks. A patient and a professor see the same entry.

Second, each record has a stable PMID. A citation that includes one can be looked up directly, and the number keeps pointing to the same record. Our guide to [what a PMID is](/blog/what-is-a-pmid) explains how to use it.

The library also maintains MeSH, the controlled vocabulary used to index articles. MeSH lets one topic be found under one heading even when different authors describe it with different words.

## How is PubMed different from a general web search engine?

A general search engine indexes the open web: news, blogs, company pages, forums and much more. PubMed is narrower and more structured.

- It covers research literature and related publications, not the general web.
- Each result is a bibliographic record with standard fields, so you can search by author, journal, title words or publication date.
- You can filter by article type, such as clinical trial, randomized controlled trial, systematic review or meta-analysis.
- Records are indexed with MeSH terms, which helps a search find articles that use different words for the same concept.
- Each record offers "Similar articles," a list of related records worth scanning when you want more on the same topic.
- Retracted papers carry a retraction notice on their record.

A general search engine may surface a news story about a study. PubMed gives you the study's own record, with its abstract and its identifiers.

## What does it mean when a paper is in PubMed?

A PubMed record shows that an article exists and gives you its details. **Whether the study is well designed, and whether its conclusions hold up, is a separate judgment.**

PubMed lists small studies and large ones, case reports and randomized controlled trials, early findings and reviews that pull many studies together. The [hierarchy of evidence](/blog/hierarchy-of-evidence) is a useful guide to how much weight different kinds of study can carry.

Check whether a record carries a retraction notice before you rely on it, and read the abstract rather than the title alone. Titles are short and can make a finding sound firmer than the paper does.

## How do you use PubMed to check a citation?

Because records are structured, PubMed is a good place to confirm that a reference is real.

1. If the citation includes a PMID, search for it. It should return exactly one record.
2. If there is no PMID, search the exact title in quotation marks, or the first author's surname with a distinctive word from the title.
3. Compare the authors, journal, year and volume with the citation.
4. Read the abstract and ask whether it supports the claim the citation is attached to.

If nothing matches after a careful search, treat the reference as unverified until you can find it some other way, such as through its DOI.

## Why does PubMed matter for AI medical answers?

AI tools can produce references that look right but point to papers that do not exist, or to real papers that do not say what the answer claims. A free, structured, publicly maintained record gives anyone a way to check.

Luma uses PubMed the same way a careful reader would. It searches PubMed for each claim in an AI answer, judges whether the evidence it finds supports the claim, and links the claim to a source only when it does. Anything it cannot verify is flagged.

## What can't PubMed do?

PubMed is a finding tool. It does not rate the quality of each study, and it does not tell you whether a finding applies to a particular person. It also does not hold every article's full text, so some papers will need a library or publisher subscription to read in full.

For health decisions, PubMed is a place to find the evidence a clinician might discuss with you, not a replacement for that conversation.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is PubMed free to use?

Yes. Anyone can search PubMed and read its citations and abstracts without paying or creating an account. Full text depends on the publisher, though many articles have free versions linked from their records.

### Who runs PubMed?

PubMed is maintained by the National Library of Medicine, which is part of the National Institutes of Health in the United States.

### Does PubMed have full-text articles?

PubMed mainly holds citations and abstracts. When a free full-text version exists, often in PubMed Central, the PubMed record links to it.

### Does being in PubMed mean a study is reliable?

No. A PubMed record confirms that an article exists and gives its details, but the quality of the study and its conclusions still have to be judged on their own.
