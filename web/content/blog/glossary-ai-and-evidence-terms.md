This glossary gives short, plain definitions of the AI and evidence terms health professionals meet most when they use AI tools and check the research behind them. Each term links to a longer explainer on this blog.

Terms are in alphabetical order. Where two terms are usually discussed together, such as relative and absolute risk, they share an explainer.

## What do the terms from A to C mean?

- [Absolute risk](/blog/relative-risk-vs-absolute-risk): the chance that an event happens to people in a group over a stated period. It shows how large a change in risk really is.
- [Abstention](/blog/abstention-when-the-right-answer-is-no-answer): an AI system declining to state a claim because the evidence is too weak to support it, and saying so plainly.
- [Abstract](/blog/how-to-read-a-pubmed-abstract): the short summary at the top of a research paper, usually covering the background, methods, results and conclusions.
- [AI hallucination](/blog/what-is-an-ai-hallucination-in-medicine): content an AI system presents as fact that is false or unsupported, including invented facts and invented sources.
- [Auditable answer](/blog/what-makes-a-medical-ai-answer-auditable): an answer that keeps a record of its claims, sources, support judgments and confidence, so someone else can review how it was reached.
- [Calibration](/blog/what-is-calibration-in-medical-ai): how closely a system's stated confidence matches how often it is actually right.
- [Claim-level verification](/blog/what-is-claim-level-verification): splitting an answer into individual factual claims and checking each one against evidence separately, instead of judging the answer as a whole.
- [Clinical practice guideline](/blog/what-is-a-clinical-practice-guideline): care recommendations written by an expert group after reviewing the evidence, and updated as the evidence changes.
- [Confidence interval](/blog/what-statistically-significant-means): a range of effect sizes reasonably compatible with a study's data. A wide interval signals an uncertain estimate.
- [Conflict of interest](/blog/conflicts-of-interest-medical-research): a financial or personal tie that could influence how research is designed, run or reported. Journals usually ask authors to disclose them.
- [Corroboration](/blog/corroboration-is-not-reproduction): agreement among sources about a finding. It is weaker evidence than an independent repeat of the experiment.

## What do the terms from D to L mean?

- [Digital object identifier (DOI)](/blog/what-is-a-doi): a permanent identifier for a published item, such as a journal article. A DOI that does not resolve to a real item is a red flag.
- [Evidence strength](/blog/what-evidence-strength-means): how much confidence a body of evidence justifies, based on study design, size, consistency and how directly it addresses the question.
- [Fabricated citation](/blog/what-is-a-fabricated-citation): a reference to a paper that does not exist. A misattributed citation, by contrast, points to a real paper that does not say what is claimed.
- [Faithfulness](/blog/why-accuracy-benchmarks-are-not-enough): whether each claim in an answer is supported by the source it cites or by the evidence the system retrieved.
- [Fine-tuning](/blog/grounding-vs-fine-tuning): further training of an AI model on a narrower set of examples, which changes what the model has learned.
- [Grounding](/blog/grounding-vs-fine-tuning): tying each answer to specific sources at the time the answer is written, so its claims can be traced and checked.
- [Hierarchy of evidence](/blog/hierarchy-of-evidence): a rough ordering of study designs by how reliably they answer questions about cause and effect, with expert opinion and laboratory work toward the bottom.
- [Human in the loop](/blog/human-in-the-loop-clinicians-ai-verification): a design in which a person reviews or approves an AI system's output before it is used.
- [Large language model](/blog/why-ai-cannot-tell-which-references-are-made-up): an AI model trained on large amounts of text to predict and generate language. Writing from memory, it works from learned patterns and does not look facts up.

## What do the terms from M to S mean?

- [Medical Subject Headings (MeSH)](/blog/how-to-use-mesh-terms): the controlled vocabulary the National Library of Medicine uses to index PubMed records by topic, so a search can match a concept as well as exact words.
- [Meta-analysis](/blog/systematic-review-vs-meta-analysis): a statistical method that combines the results of several studies on the same question into one overall estimate.
- [Model Context Protocol](/blog/what-is-model-context-protocol-health-ai): an open standard that lets AI assistants call outside tools and data sources.
- [Observational study](/blog/observational-studies-what-they-can-tell-you): a study that watches what happens without assigning treatments, such as a cohort, case-control or cross-sectional study. On its own, it shows association, not cause and effect.
- [Peer review](/blog/what-peer-review-checks): evaluation of a manuscript by independent experts before a journal publishes it.
- [Preprint](/blog/what-is-a-preprint): a research paper shared publicly before peer review. Its conclusions should be treated as provisional.
- [Provenance](/blog/what-is-provenance-in-ai): the traceable origin of a statement. In AI, it means each claim can be followed back to its source.
- [PubMed](/blog/what-is-pubmed): a free search engine for biomedical literature, maintained by the National Library of Medicine at the National Institutes of Health. It holds citations and abstracts.
- [PubMed Central](/blog/pubmed-central-vs-pubmed): the National Library of Medicine's free archive of full-text journal articles.
- [PubMed ID (PMID)](/blog/what-is-a-pmid): the unique number PubMed assigns to each record, and a dependable anchor for checking a citation.
- [Randomized controlled trial](/blog/what-is-a-randomized-controlled-trial): a study that assigns participants by chance to a treatment group or a comparison group, which lets it test cause and effect.
- [Relative risk](/blog/relative-risk-vs-absolute-risk): the risk in one group divided by the risk in another. It shows proportional change and can make a small absolute difference look large.
- [Replication](/blog/why-one-study-is-rarely-the-final-word): repeating a study independently, with new participants, to see whether the result holds.
- [Retraction](/blog/how-to-check-if-a-paper-was-retracted): the formal withdrawal of a published paper, for example because of serious errors. PubMed shows retraction notices on affected records.
- [Retrieval-augmented generation](/blog/does-retrieval-augmented-generation-stop-hallucinations): a technique in which an AI system retrieves documents at answer time and writes from them. Retrieved sources are real, but the system can still misstate what they say.
- [Statistical significance](/blog/what-statistically-significant-means): a label for a result that would be unlikely if there were no real effect. It does not mean the effect is large or important.
- [Systematic review](/blog/systematic-review-vs-meta-analysis): a review that finds, appraises and summarizes the studies on a question using a stated, repeatable method.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between a fabricated citation and a misattributed one?

A fabricated citation points to a paper that does not exist. A misattributed citation points to a real paper that does not support the claim it is attached to.

### What does grounding mean in AI?

Grounding means tying an AI answer to specific sources at the time the answer is written. Each claim can then be traced back to a source and checked.

### What is the difference between PubMed and PubMed Central?

PubMed is a free search engine of citations and abstracts for biomedical literature. PubMed Central is a free archive of full-text articles, and many PubMed records link to it.

### Why does calibration matter in medical AI?

People act on how confident a system sounds. A well-calibrated system is right about as often as its confidence suggests, so its certainty carries real information.
