Abstention is a system's decision to withhold a claim, or a whole answer, when the evidence does not support it well enough. In medical AI, a good abstention can be the most honest output available, because it shows the user exactly where the evidence runs out instead of filling the gap with a guess.

Getting abstention right takes two design decisions. You need a threshold that decides when to withhold, and a presentation that makes the withheld claim useful instead of a dead end.

## Why do medical AI systems need to abstain?

Language models generate fluent text whether or not they have good grounds for it. Nothing in the generation process pauses when the model is uncertain, so uncertainty has to be handled by the system around the model. [Why AI tools rarely say "I don't know"](/blog/why-ai-tools-rarely-say-i-dont-know) explains the mechanics.

Citations show the stakes. An audit published in The Lancet in 2026 reports that prior studies estimate 30 to 69 percent of references generated by large language models in biomedical contexts are fabricated. A system with no way to abstain has no honest option when it cannot find support, so it fills the gap.

## Should a system abstain on a whole answer or on single claims?

On single claims, for most questions. Whole-answer abstention throws away the supported parts along with the unsupported one. If most of an answer's claims are well supported and one is not, the user is better served by the sourced claims plus one clear flag than by a refusal.

Whole-answer abstention still has a place. Some requests fall outside what any evidence check can responsibly answer, such as interpreting one person's test result or recommending a dose. Those should be declined with a clear reason and a pointer to a clinician.

## How do you set a threshold for withholding a claim?

A threshold is the line between "supported enough to show with a source" and "flag instead." Set it deliberately, in this order.

### 1. Define support before you tune anything

Decide what counts as support. The evidence should address the same population, the same intervention or exposure, and the same outcome as the claim, with the same direction of effect. A paper that only shares keywords with the claim does not count, however relevant it looks.

### 2. Build a labeled set of claims

Collect claims from your real use cases. Have people with clinical or research training mark each one as supported, not supported, or contradicted by the evidence. Include easy cases, hard cases and claims on topics where the literature is thin.

### 3. Decide which error costs more

Showing an unsupported claim as supported can mislead a reader about their health. Flagging a claim that was in fact supported costs the reader time and some trust in the flags. In health information the first error is usually the more harmful one, so the threshold should lean toward flagging.

### 4. Measure both kinds of error

Track how often the system shows claims your reviewers marked unsupported, and how often it flags claims they marked supported. Tuning against only one of these produces a system that is either reckless or useless.

### 5. Check calibration

If the system gives a confidence score, check that claims scored higher really are supported more often. A threshold set on a poorly calibrated score will not behave the way you expect. [What calibration is](/blog/what-is-calibration-in-medical-ai) covers this in plain terms.

### 6. Re-run the set when anything changes

A new model, a new prompt or a change to retrieval can shift where the threshold lands. Treat the labeled set as a regression test and run it before every release.

## What does over-abstention look like?

A system that flags everything is safe on paper and useless in practice. Users learn to ignore the flags, which defeats their purpose. Watch for these signs:

- Flags landing on textbook-level, well-documented claims.
- Flags caused by retrieval failures, such as a search that used the wrong vocabulary, rather than by a real absence of evidence.
- Expert reviewers disagreeing with the flags more often than they agree.

When the flags miss, improve retrieval and the definition of support before you loosen the threshold. A looser threshold hides the problem and lets genuinely unsupported claims through with it.

## How should an abstention be presented?

An abstention should tell the user what happened and what to do next. A useful one includes:

- **The claim itself, still visible.** Do not delete it silently. The user needs to know it was in the answer and was not verified.
- **A short reason.** "No relevant studies found," "studies found, but in a different population" and "evidence points the other way" are different situations and should read differently.
- **What was searched.** Naming the source, such as PubMed, tells the user the scope of the check.
- **A next step.** For a professional user, that might be search terms to continue with. For a patient, it is a prompt to ask their clinician.
- **Plain wording.** One clear line works better than a paragraph of hedging or reassurance.

Visual design matters as much as wording. A flag in fine print reads as a footnote, and a claim that looks identical to its supported neighbors reads as checked. ## How do you know abstention is working?

Review a sample of flagged claims on a regular schedule with someone who knows the subject. Each flag should be explainable: the reviewer should agree that the evidence was missing, mismatched or contradictory.

Record the reason behind each flag over time. If flags caused by missed searches start to rise, fix retrieval first. If reviewers keep agreeing with the flags and users keep acting on them, the threshold is doing its job.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is abstention in AI?
Abstention is when an AI system withholds a claim or an answer because it lacks enough support to state it. In medical AI, it usually appears as a visible flag on a claim the system could not verify.

### Does abstention make an AI tool less useful?
Not when it is designed well. A clear flag on one unsupported claim, with a reason and a next step, tells users where to look harder, while the supported claims keep their sources.

### How do you choose an abstention threshold?
Define what counts as support, label a set of real claims with expert reviewers, and set the line based on which error costs more. In health information, showing an unsupported claim is usually worse than flagging a supported one.

### Should a medical AI ever refuse to answer entirely?
Yes, when the request falls outside what an evidence check can answer, such as interpreting one person's test result or recommending a dose. The refusal should give a clear reason and point the user to a clinician.
