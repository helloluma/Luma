AI tools rarely say "I don't know" because nothing in the way they generate text requires them to check whether they know something before they answer. A large language model writes by producing plausible next words, and a fluent answer is plausible whether or not the facts behind it are right.

Saying "I don't know" has to be built into a system on purpose. Left to itself, the model keeps writing.

## How does an AI model produce an answer?

A large language model learns patterns from a very large body of text. When you ask it a question, it produces a response one small piece at a time, each piece chosen because it plausibly follows what came before.

In ordinary writing, questions are usually followed by answers. So the expected continuation of a question is an answer, delivered in the steady register people use when they explain things. The model produces that shape of text whether the content underneath reflects a well-established fact, a loose pattern or a blend of several sources.

There is no separate step where the model looks up the fact, notices it is missing and decides to stop. Any step like that has to be added from outside.

## Why does an AI answer sound equally sure when it is wrong?

People signal doubt in how they speak. They hedge, pause, qualify and say "I think." Readers learn to hear those signals and adjust their trust.

Model output does not carry those signals reliably. A correct sentence and an invented one can come out in the same polished tone, with the same grammar and the same air of authority. The reader loses one of the main cues they use to decide whether to believe a statement. We look at this effect in [why fluent writing makes AI medical errors harder to spot](/blog/why-fluent-ai-writing-hides-errors).

## Can't you just ask the model how sure it is?

You can ask, and you will get an answer. The trouble is that the answer is more generated text. A model can say it is very confident about a claim it got wrong, or hedge on one it got right.

Whether a system's stated confidence matches how often it is actually right is called calibration, and it has to be measured on real questions. [What calibration means for medical AI](/blog/what-is-calibration-in-medical-ai) explains how to think about it.

Asking a model to double-check its own references runs into the same wall. A model writing from memory has no separate record of what it read, so a second pass is another round of generation. We explain why in [why AI models cannot tell you which of their references they made up](/blog/why-ai-cannot-tell-which-references-are-made-up).

## Does grading AI on right answers make the problem worse?

It can. If a system is judged only on how many questions it answers correctly, a guess sometimes scores and "I don't know" never does. A system tuned to do well on that kind of test is pushed toward answering every time.

Health care needs a different scoring rule. A confident wrong answer about a drug interaction is worse than no answer, because the reader may act on it. A useful evaluation counts an honest abstention as better than a wrong answer.

## Why does this matter more in medicine?

In many areas, a confident wrong answer wastes time. In health, it can shape a decision about care, a patient handout or a published paper.

Invented sources are a clear case. In an audit published in The Lancet in 2026, Topaz and colleagues report that prior studies estimate 30 to 69 percent of references generated by large language models in biomedical contexts are fabricated. A fabricated reference gives no sign that it is fabricated until someone looks it up.

The same problem applies to the claims themselves. A claim about a side effect or a risk factor can arrive with total assurance and have nothing behind it. The reader cannot tell the difference from the text alone.

## How do you design abstention into a medical AI system?

Abstention means a system declines to state or support a claim when the evidence is not there. It has to be built as a deliberate step:

1. **Check claims against an outside source.** Compare each factual claim with published research retrieved at answer time instead of trusting the model's memory.
2. **Set a support threshold.** Decide what counts as enough evidence to present a claim as supported, and treat anything below it as unsupported.
3. **Show the flag.** Tell the reader which claims are unsupported, in plain words, next to the claim itself.
4. **Separate "no evidence found" from "evidence disagrees."** They are different findings, and readers should be able to tell them apart.
5. **Score abstention as a good outcome.** When you test the system, count an honest "not supported" as better than a wrong answer with a citation.

Luma follows the same principle. It splits an answer into claims, searches PubMed for each one and flags what it cannot verify instead of attaching a citation the evidence does not back. ## What should you do when an AI answer sounds certain?

A few habits help:

- Treat the tone as style. Confident wording is not evidence of accuracy.
- Ask where each important claim comes from, then check the source yourself.
- Be most careful with specific details: names, numbers, references and anything about a particular drug or condition.
- Notice when a question has no settled answer. A confident reply to an open question is a warning sign.
- For questions about your own health, take the answer to your clinician as a starting point for a conversation.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why do AI chatbots make things up instead of saying they don't know?

A large language model generates plausible text and has no built-in step that checks whether it knows a fact before answering. Unless a system adds that check from outside, the model produces an answer-shaped response either way.

### Can an AI model tell when it is wrong?

Not reliably on its own. Asking a model how confident it is, or asking it to double-check itself, produces more generated text, so real checking has to happen against an outside source such as published research.

### Is an AI answer more reliable when it sounds confident?

No. AI output can sound equally confident whether it is right or wrong, so the tone of an answer is not evidence of its accuracy.

### What is abstention in AI?

Abstention is when a system declines to give or support an answer because the evidence is not strong enough. In medical AI, it can take the form of a clear flag that a claim is not supported.
