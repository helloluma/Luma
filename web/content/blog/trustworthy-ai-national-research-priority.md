The National Library of Medicine, part of the National Institutes of Health, lists Advancing Trustworthy, Reproducible, and Rigorous Biomedical AI first among its four research priority areas. The case for it is easy to see: AI systems now write and summarize biomedical text, and the published record already shows what happens when errors go unchecked.

Below is what the priority areas say, what those three qualities look like in a working system, and why they matter to anyone who reads, writes or builds with medical information.

## What are the National Library of Medicine's research priority areas?

The library names four, in this order:

1. Advancing Trustworthy, Reproducible, and Rigorous Biomedical AI
2. Biomedical Data Infrastructure at Scale
3. Sustainable Biomedical Reference Resources and Platform Science
4. Biomedical Tools and Methods, Human-Centered Use and Impact

The National Library of Medicine maintains PubMed, PubMed Central and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), resources that researchers, writers and clinicians use to find and cite evidence. When the institution behind those resources lists trustworthy AI first, the ordering deserves attention. [What the National Library of Medicine does](/blog/what-the-national-library-of-medicine-does) covers its roles in more detail.

## What does trustworthy, reproducible and rigorous AI look like in practice?

The priority's title names three qualities. The working definitions below are ours, written for a system that answers medical questions.

A trustworthy answer shows you why it should be believed. Each factual claim is tied to a source a reader can open, and claims without support are marked as unsupported instead of being presented as fact. The ability to trace a statement back to its source is what [provenance in AI](/blog/what-is-provenance-in-ai) means.

Reproducible means someone else can run the same check and see the same evidence. That requires a record of what was searched, what was retrieved and how each judgment was made. A system whose method is hidden cannot be reproduced by anyone outside it.

Rigor means checking that a source supports a claim, beyond checking that the source exists. A real paper can be cited for a finding it does not report. A rigorous system compares each claim with what the evidence actually says, and it is tested on questions where the right answer is already known.

## Why does trustworthy AI matter so much in biomedicine?

Errors in biomedical text travel. A wrong claim in one paper can be cited by the next, repeated in teaching materials and passed along to patients. Each step makes the error look more established.

The published record already shows the problem. An audit by Topaz and colleagues in The Lancet in 2026 checked 97.1 million structured references across about 2.5 million biomedical papers and found 4,046 fabricated references across 2,810 papers. Roughly one in 277 papers published in the first seven weeks of 2026 cited a paper that does not exist, up from one in 458 in 2025 and one in 2,828 in 2023.

The audit also reports that prior studies estimate 30 to 69 percent of references generated by large language models in biomedical contexts are fabricated, and that 98.4 percent of affected papers had received no publisher action at the time of the audit. When fabricated references can enter the record and stay there, trust has to be built into AI systems on purpose. It will not appear on its own.

## How do the other three priority areas connect to trustworthy AI?

Read together, the four areas describe a chain. This is our reading of how the links fit:

- **Biomedical Data Infrastructure at Scale.** An AI system is only as reliable as the data it draws on. Checking a claim requires dependable access to the records you are checking against.
- **Sustainable Biomedical Reference Resources and Platform Science.** Verification needs something to verify against. Reference resources such as PubMed and MeSH are that foundation for the published literature, and they have to stay maintained and open for checks to keep working.
- **Biomedical Tools and Methods, Human-Centered Use and Impact.** A check helps only if people can see it and act on it. A flag buried where no one looks changes nothing.

Trustworthy AI sits at the top of that chain. It depends on the other three being in place.

## What does this mean for people building health AI?

A few design choices follow from these goals, and any team can make them now:

- Tie each factual claim to a public source anyone can check.
- Show unsupported claims as unsupported. Never attach a citation the evidence does not back.
- Keep a record of what was checked, what was found and what was flagged, so answers can be audited later. [What makes a medical AI answer auditable](/blog/what-makes-a-medical-ai-answer-auditable) lists what that record should hold.
- Publish your method so others can inspect it and repeat it.
- Test on questions with known answers, and count an honest "not supported" as a good result.

Luma is built around these choices. It checks AI-generated medical answers claim by claim against PubMed, links each supported claim to its source, flags what it cannot verify, and is [open source](/blog/why-luma-is-open-source) under the Apache 2.0 license so anyone can inspect how the checks work.

## What does this mean for readers?

For anyone reading AI-generated medical text, the same three qualities make a useful test. Ask whether you can see the source behind each claim, whether you could check it yourself and whether the answer tells you when something is unsupported. An answer that fails all three asks for trust it has not earned.
