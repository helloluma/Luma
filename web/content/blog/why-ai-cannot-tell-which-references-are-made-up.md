An AI model writing from memory cannot tell you which of its references it made up, because it keeps no separate record of the papers it learned from. A reference it invents and a reference it remembers come out of the same process, so from the inside they look the same.

Asking the model to double-check its citations is therefore not verification. The only reliable check happens outside the model, against the source itself.

## How does a language model produce a reference?

A large language model writes by predicting likely text, one piece at a time, based on patterns in what it was trained on. A reference has a very regular pattern: author surnames, a title in a familiar style, a journal name, a year, a volume and page numbers.

The model has seen enough references to produce that pattern fluently. Sometimes the result matches a real paper. Sometimes it blends pieces of several papers, or produces a title and author list that fit the topic but belong to nothing. The [explainer on fabricated citations](/blog/what-is-a-fabricated-citation) covers the difference between invented and misattributed references.

## Why doesn't the model know which references are real?

The model does not store its training text as a library it can search. What it keeps is a large set of learned patterns. It has no catalog of documents with titles and identifiers to consult. When it writes a reference from memory, there is no step where it looks the paper up and confirms it exists.

So the model has no reliable way to separate "I remember this paper" from "this looks like a paper on this topic." Both produce fluent, specific, confident text. **The confidence of the wording tells you nothing about whether the paper exists.**

## What happens when you ask the model to check itself?

You get more generated text. The model may confirm the reference, apologize and swap in a different one, or describe the paper's findings in convincing detail. None of those responses comes from looking anything up, unless the system is actually connected to a search tool and uses it.

A second pass can sometimes catch an obvious inconsistency inside the answer. It cannot establish that a paper exists, because the same memory that produced the reference is doing the checking.

## How often do AI models invent references?

Often enough that every reference needs checking. Two studies give a sense of scale.

Walters and Wilder, writing in Scientific Reports in 2023, examined 636 citations across 84 literature reviews generated by an earlier version and a newer version of a widely used chatbot, covering 42 topics from many fields rather than medicine alone. Of the earlier version's citations, 55 percent were fabricated. Of the newer version's, 18 percent were. Among the citations that were real, 43 percent of the earlier version's and 24 percent of the newer version's contained substantive errors.

A 2026 audit in The Lancet by Topaz and colleagues reports that prior studies estimate 30 to 69 percent of references generated by large language models in biomedical contexts are fabricated. The same audit found that roughly one in 277 papers published in the first seven weeks of 2026 cited a paper that does not exist, up from one in 458 in 2025 and one in 2,828 in 2023.

## Does connecting the model to search fix the problem?

It helps with one part. A system that retrieves real papers at answer time and cites only what it retrieved is citing records that exist. That approach is called retrieval-augmented generation.

It does not settle whether those records support the claims. The model can still misstate what a retrieved paper says, attach the right paper to the wrong sentence, or fill gaps from memory. The explainer on [what retrieval-augmented generation can and cannot fix](/blog/does-retrieval-augmented-generation-stop-hallucinations) goes further, and the short version is that each claim still needs its own check against its source.

## Where does real verification happen?

Against the record. For each reference:

1. Look up the PubMed ID (PMID) in PubMed, or resolve the digital object identifier (DOI).
2. If there is no identifier, search PubMed for the exact title and the first author.
3. Confirm that the title, authors, journal, year and volume match.
4. Read the abstract and ask whether it supports the specific sentence the reference is attached to.

More detail is in [how to check if a PubMed citation is real](/blog/how-to-check-if-a-pubmed-citation-is-real). Software can do the first pass at scale. Luma, for example, splits an answer into claims, searches PubMed for each one, links the claims the evidence supports to their sources and flags the rest. You can watch it work on the [demo](/demo).

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I ask an AI chatbot whether its citations are real?

You can, but its answer is not a check. Unless it actually searches a database, the model is generating another response from the same memory that produced the citation.

### Why do fake AI references look so convincing?

They follow the exact format of real references, with plausible authors, journals and titles on the right topic. The model learned that pattern well, so the fakes come out fluent and specific.

### Do newer AI models still make up references?

In a 2023 study of a widely used chatbot, the newer version fabricated 18 percent of its citations, down from 55 percent for the earlier version. Fewer is not none, so every reference still needs checking.

### What is the quickest way to check an AI-generated reference?

Search PubMed for its PubMed ID, or for its exact title if no identifier is given. If no matching record turns up, or the details do not match, treat the reference as unverified.
