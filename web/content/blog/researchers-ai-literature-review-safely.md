Researchers can use AI safely in a literature review by keeping it on tasks where its output can be checked against something they control, such as brainstorming search terms or summarizing papers they have already found and read. The unsafe use is asking it to find or cite papers from its own memory, because it can produce references to papers that do not exist.

The workflow below keeps the AI in a supporting role and keeps you in charge of the evidence.

## Where does AI help in a literature review?

AI tools work best on text you give them and on generating options you can judge. In a review, that makes them useful for:

- Brainstorming search terms: synonyms, related concepts, alternate spellings and terms used in neighboring fields.
- Drafting a search strategy: suggesting how to combine concepts with AND and OR, which you then test and refine in the database yourself.
- Suggesting subject headings: candidate Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) to look up, which you confirm in the [MeSH database](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/mesh/) before using.
- Summarizing papers you supply: condensing an abstract or full text you have pasted in, so you can triage faster.
- Organizing notes: grouping your extracted findings by theme, outcome or study design.
- Editing prose: tightening a draft you wrote.

In each case, the output is either something you will test, like a search term, or a transformation of a source you already hold, like a summary.

## Where does AI fail in a literature review?

The risk rises as soon as the AI becomes the source.

- Inventing papers: asked for references, a model can produce citations with realistic authors, journals and years for papers that were never published.
- Misattributing findings: it can pair a real paper with a claim the paper does not make.
- Summarizing what it has not read: asked about a paper by title alone, a model may describe it from patterns rather than from the paper itself.
- Smoothing over uncertainty: summaries can drop caveats and turn "may be associated with" into "causes."
- Missing evidence: a model's memory is not a systematic search, and it cannot tell you what it left out.

Walters and Wilder studied this problem directly in a paper published in Scientific Reports in 2023. They examined 636 citations across 84 literature reviews on 42 topics, generated by an earlier version and a newer version of a widely used chatbot. The topics came from many fields, not only medicine.

In their results, 55 percent of the citations from the earlier version and 18 percent from the newer version were fabricated. Among the citations that were real, 43 percent of the earlier version's and 24 percent of the newer version's contained substantive errors. **Even the better result means an AI-generated reference list cannot be used without checking every entry.** We look at the study in more depth in [what a 2023 study of chatbot citations found](/blog/what-the-2023-chatbot-citation-study-found).

## What does a safe AI-assisted workflow look like?

### 1. Define the question yourself

Write your review question and your inclusion and exclusion criteria before you open an AI tool. A clear question keeps the tool on supporting tasks and makes its suggestions easier to judge.

### 2. Use AI to widen your search terms

Ask for synonyms and related terms, then test each one in PubMed. Keep the terms that return relevant records and drop the rest. Check any suggested subject heading in the MeSH database, since a model can propose headings that do not exist. Our guide on [how to use MeSH terms to search PubMed](/blog/how-to-use-mesh-terms) explains how.

### 3. Run the searches in the database

Every paper in your review should come from a database search you ran, or from a reference you followed to its source. Record each search string, the database, the date you ran it and how many results it returned, so anyone can repeat the search.

### 4. Screen the results yourself

You can ask AI to summarize abstracts you have retrieved to speed up a first pass. Make the inclusion decisions yourself, against your written criteria, and note the reason for each exclusion.

### 5. Verify every reference

Before any citation goes into your draft, look it up by its PubMed ID (PMID) or digital object identifier (DOI) and confirm the authors, title, journal and year. If AI helped draft a passage, check every reference in it, including ones that look familiar. Our guide on [how to check if a PubMed citation is real](/blog/how-to-check-if-a-pubmed-citation-is-real) covers the steps.

### 6. Check each claim against its source

A real reference can still be attached to the wrong claim. For each sentence that cites a paper, read the abstract or the full text and confirm it says what your sentence says, in the same population and with the same level of certainty.

### 7. Keep a log

Note where AI was used: which tool, for which task, and how you checked the output. A log makes it easy to disclose AI use if your journal or institution asks, and it lets a co-author see exactly what was checked and how.

## How should you treat AI-generated summaries?

Treat them as notes, not as evidence. A summary of a paper you supplied is only as good as its match to that paper.

Spot-check summaries against the source, especially for numbers, population details and the strength of the conclusions. Never cite a summary. Cite the paper, after you have read the part of it that supports your sentence. Our post on [using AI to summarize medical research](/blog/using-ai-to-summarize-medical-research) lists what to double-check.

## What should you never hand to AI?

Some parts of a review need a human with access to the sources.

- Deciding which studies to include.
- Judging the quality or risk of bias of a study you have not read.
- Supplying references that you have not found yourself.
- Writing conclusions that go beyond the evidence you collected.

Keeping those tasks with the research team is what makes the rest of the AI use safe.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I use AI to write a literature review?

You can use AI for supporting tasks such as brainstorming search terms, summarizing papers you have already retrieved, and editing your own prose. The searching, the selection of studies and the checking of every reference should be your own work.

### Why do AI tools make up references?

Language models generate text from learned patterns rather than looking papers up, so they can produce citations that look real but match no published work. A 2023 study by Walters and Wilder found fabricated citations in literature reviews generated by both an earlier and a newer version of a widely used chatbot.

### How do I check a reference an AI tool suggested?

Search its PMID, DOI or exact title on PubMed, confirm that the authors, journal and year match, and read the abstract to make sure it supports your claim. If you cannot find it, do not cite it.

### Should I disclose AI use in a literature review?

Follow your journal's or institution's policy, and keep a log of which tools you used and for what. A log makes disclosure simple and shows how you checked the output.
