To verify an AI-generated reference list, take each reference in turn: resolve its identifier, confirm that the authors, title, journal and year match the record, read the abstract against the sentence the reference is meant to support, and record what you found. A reference passes only when it exists, matches and supports the claim it is attached to.

## Why does every AI-generated reference need checking?

Large language models can produce references that look exactly like real ones. An audit of biomedical papers by Topaz and colleagues, [published in The Lancet in 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42107362/), reports that prior studies estimate 30 to 69 percent of references generated by large language models in biomedical contexts are fabricated.

Invented references also reach print. The same audit found that roughly one in 277 papers published in the first seven weeks of 2026 cited a paper that does not exist, up from one in 458 in 2025 and one in 2,828 in 2023.

Real references can fail as well, by carrying the wrong details or by pointing to a paper that does not say what the sentence claims. So each reference has to pass three tests: it exists, it matches, and it supports. For checking a single citation, see [how to check if a PubMed citation is real](/blog/how-to-check-if-a-pubmed-citation-is-real). The procedure below scales that up to a whole list.

## What do you need before you start?

Gather three things:

- The full reference list, exactly as the AI tool produced it
- The text with every citation marker in place, so you know which sentence each reference supports
- A log, in a document or spreadsheet, with one entry per reference

For each entry, plan to record the reference number, the sentence it supports, the identifier given, the record you found, whether the details match, whether the source supports the sentence, the action you took and the date you checked.

## What are the steps to verify an AI reference list?

### Step 1: Map each reference to the sentence it supports

Number every reference. Next to each one, copy the exact sentence or sentences that cite it. Note any reference that no sentence cites, and any factual sentence that has no reference. Both are worth fixing before you go further.

### Step 2: Resolve each identifier

- For a PubMed ID (PMID), search it on [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). A real PMID opens one specific record.
- For a digital object identifier (DOI), resolve it through the [DOI system](https://www.doi.org/). A real DOI leads to the publisher's page for one specific article. The article on [what a DOI is](/blog/what-is-a-doi) explains how resolution works.
- If there is no identifier, search the exact title on PubMed, then try the first author's surname with distinctive words from the title.

A DOI that does not resolve, or a PMID that opens a different paper, is a strong sign of a fabricated or garbled reference. An invented PMID can still open a real record, just for an unrelated paper, so always compare what you find with what was cited.

### Step 3: Match the metadata

Compare the record with the reference, field by field:

- Authors, including the first author and the order
- Article title
- Journal name
- Year, volume, issue and pages

Small formatting differences are normal. A different journal, a different year, a different first author or a title that is close but not identical are warning signs. If the paper is clearly the same one and only details are off, correct the reference from the record. If no matching paper turns up, treat the reference as fabricated.

While you have the record open, look for a retraction notice. The steps are in [how to check whether a paper has been retracted](/blog/how-to-check-if-a-paper-was-retracted).

### Step 4: Read the abstract against the sentence

**A paper that exists can still fail to support the sentence it is attached to.** Read the abstract with the sentence beside it and ask:

- Is it the same population?
- Is it the same intervention or exposure?
- Is it the same outcome?
- Does the effect go in the same direction?
- Is the sentence as cautious as the paper, or more certain?
- Is the finding the paper's own result, or something mentioned only in its background?

The checklist in [how to tell whether a study actually supports a claim](/blog/how-to-tell-if-a-study-supports-a-claim) covers each question in more detail. If the abstract does not settle it, read the full text. [PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) has free full text for many papers.

### Step 5: Record a verdict and act on it

Give each reference one outcome in your log:

1. Verified: it exists, the details match and it supports the sentence.
2. Corrected: it is real and supports the sentence, but you fixed its details from the record.
3. Unsupported: it is real but does not support the sentence as written. Rewrite the sentence to match the source, or find a source that does support it.
4. Not found: no matching paper exists. Remove it.
5. Retracted: remove it as support for the claim.

## What should you do with claims left without a source?

Removing a bad reference leaves a claim with nothing behind it. Do not attach the nearest paper that sounds related. Search for real evidence, rewrite the claim to match what that evidence says, or cut it.

If you cannot resolve a claim before a deadline, mark it clearly as unverified rather than letting it pass. A claim that cannot be supported should be flagged, not given a citation.

## How can you speed this up without cutting corners?

- Resolve all the identifiers first. Quick failures sort the list and show you where to spend time.
- Work through every reference in the same order and record the same fields, so the log is easy to review.
- Ask the AI tool to include identifiers, but never accept its statement that it has checked them. A model reviewing its own references has nothing outside itself to check them against.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I check if an AI-generated reference is real?

Look up its PubMed ID on PubMed or resolve its DOI at doi.org, then confirm that the authors, title, journal and year match the record. If no identifier is given, search the exact title on PubMed.

### How many AI-generated references are fabricated?

An audit of biomedical papers by Topaz and colleagues in The Lancet in 2026 reports that prior studies estimate 30 to 69 percent of references generated by large language models in biomedical contexts are fabricated. That range is the reason every reference needs checking.

### Is it enough to confirm that a cited paper exists?

No. A real paper can still fail to support the sentence it is attached to, so read the abstract, and the full text when needed, against the specific claim.

### What should I do with a reference I cannot find?

Treat it as fabricated and remove it. Then treat the claim it supported as unsourced until you find real evidence for it or rewrite the claim to match the evidence you have.
