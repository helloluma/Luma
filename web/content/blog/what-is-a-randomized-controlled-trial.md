A randomized controlled trial is a study in which participants are assigned by chance to receive either the treatment being tested or a comparison, such as a placebo or the usual treatment, and the groups are then followed to see how their outcomes differ. It matters because chance assignment tends to make the groups similar in every way except the treatment, so a difference in outcomes is more likely to be caused by the treatment itself.

For questions about whether a treatment works, randomized controlled trials usually sit near the top of the [hierarchy of evidence](/blog/hierarchy-of-evidence).

## What does "randomized" mean?

Randomized means each participant's group is decided by chance, for example by a computer-generated sequence, rather than by the researchers or the participants.

Chance assignment matters because people differ in many ways: age, overall health, habits, genetics, and factors no one thinks to measure. If doctors chose who got a new treatment, they might, without meaning to, pick patients who were healthier to begin with. The treatment would then look better than it really is.

Randomization spreads both known and unknown differences across the groups. **On average, the groups start out alike**, so they can be compared fairly.

## What is the control group for?

The control group shows what happens without the treatment being tested. Symptoms change over time for many reasons: some illnesses get better on their own, measurements vary from day to day, and people can feel better simply from taking part in a study. Without a comparison group, any of these could be mistaken for a treatment effect.

Depending on the question, the control group may receive:

- A placebo, an inactive look-alike of the treatment
- The current standard treatment, so the new one is compared with what people would otherwise get
- No treatment, in some trials

## What is blinding?

Blinding means keeping people unaware of who is getting which treatment. In a single-blind trial, the participants do not know their group. In a double-blind trial, neither the participants nor the researchers who assess them know.

Blinding protects against expectations. A participant who knows they are getting the new treatment may report feeling better. A researcher who knows may judge a borderline result more favorably. Neither has to be dishonest for this to happen.

Blinding is not always possible. It is hard to hide whether someone had surgery or took a pill, for example. Trials that cannot be blinded are more open to this kind of bias, and good trial reports say so.

## Why can a randomized controlled trial show cause and effect?

Many kinds of study can show that two things go together. Fewer can show that one causes the other.

In an observational study, researchers watch what people already do. People who choose a habit may differ in other ways that also affect their health, and those differences, called confounders, can create a link that is not causal. Our post on [what observational studies can and cannot tell you](/blog/observational-studies-what-they-can-tell-you) explains this in more detail.

A randomized controlled trial takes the choice away. Because chance decides who gets the treatment, the groups should differ only in the treatment and in random variation. When a well-run trial finds a clear difference between groups, the treatment is the most likely explanation.

## What are the limits of randomized controlled trials?

Trials are powerful, but they are not perfect, and a single trial is rarely the last word. Common limits include:

- Who took part: trials often enroll specific groups and exclude people with other conditions, so the results may not apply to everyone.
- Size: small trials can miss real effects or produce findings that are due to chance.
- Length: a trial follows people for a set period, so effects that take longer to appear may not show up.
- Rare side effects: a side effect that affects very few people may not appear until a treatment is used much more widely.
- What was measured: some trials measure a lab value rather than an outcome patients feel or care about.
- Ethics: some questions cannot be tested this way. Researchers cannot randomly assign people to smoke, for example, so evidence about the harms of smoking comes from other kinds of study.

Results from a single trial also need to be repeated and weighed alongside other trials. Our post on [why one study is rarely the final word](/blog/why-one-study-is-rarely-the-final-word) covers that, and [systematic review versus meta-analysis](/blog/systematic-review-vs-meta-analysis) explains how researchers combine many trials on the same question.

## How do you tell if a study is a randomized controlled trial?

Look in the abstract or the methods section for words such as "randomized," "randomly assigned" or "allocated at random." On PubMed, the free database of biomedical research maintained by the National Library of Medicine, you can filter search results by article type, and randomized controlled trial is one of the types.

The word "trial" on its own does not guarantee randomization. Some trials have a single group with no comparison, and some compare patients with past records instead of a randomized control group. Those designs can be useful, but they do not carry the same protection against bias.

## What should patients take from this?

Knowing what a randomized controlled trial is can help you read health news and ask better questions. When you see a headline about a new treatment, it is fair to ask three things. Was it tested in a randomized controlled trial? Were the people in the trial similar to you? Did the trial measure outcomes that matter to you?

Your clinician can help you understand whether a particular trial applies to your situation. A trial result describes what happened to groups of people. Whether it fits your own care is a question for the clinician who knows your history.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a randomized controlled trial in simple terms?

It is a study that uses chance to decide which participants get a treatment and which get a comparison, then compares what happens to each group. Using chance makes the groups similar, so differences in outcomes can be linked to the treatment.

### Why is randomization important?

Randomization spreads known and unknown differences between people across the groups, so on average the groups start out alike. That keeps choices made by researchers or participants from creating a false difference between treatments.

### What is a double-blind trial?

In a double-blind trial, neither the participants nor the researchers assessing them know who is receiving which treatment. Blinding reduces the chance that expectations influence how results are reported or judged.

### Are randomized controlled trials always the best evidence?

For questions about whether a treatment works, well-run randomized controlled trials and reviews that combine them are usually the strongest evidence. For some questions, such as rare long-term harms or exposures that cannot ethically be assigned, other study designs are needed.
