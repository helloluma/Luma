Animal and lab studies can show that something is biologically possible, but they cannot show that it will happen in people. A result in cells or mice describes what happened in that model, and whether it changes health in humans has to be tested in humans.

These studies are an essential part of medical research. The trouble starts when their findings are reported as if they were findings about people.

## What are animal and lab studies?

Researchers often call them preclinical studies, because they come before testing in people. There are two broad kinds:

- **In vitro studies** are done on cells, tissues or molecules outside a living body, in a dish or a test tube. In vitro is Latin for "in glass."
- **Animal studies** are done in living animals, such as mice and rats. They are one kind of in vivo study, meaning a study in a living organism.

Both kinds answer questions that are hard or impossible to answer in people. They can show how a molecule interacts with a receptor, what happens inside a cell when a gene is switched off, or whether a new compound is toxic at high exposures. They also help researchers decide which ideas are promising enough to test in people.

## Why don't results in cells carry over to people?

A dish lacks almost everything a body adds. Several differences stand between a cell culture and a person:

- **Concentration.** Cells in a dish can be exposed directly to amounts of a substance that might never reach them inside a body.
- **Everything the body does.** In a person, a substance has to be absorbed, carried in the blood, broken down and cleared. The immune system and other organs respond along the way.
- **The cells themselves.** Cell lines grown in labs can behave differently from the same type of cell inside a living person.

A textbook illustration: plenty of substances kill cancer cells in a dish, including some that would harm a person at the same exposure. Killing cells in a dish shows that a substance is active. It does not show that the substance is a safe or useful treatment.

## Why don't results in animals carry over to people?

Animals are closer to people than cells are, but the gap is still wide:

- **Species differences.** Animals and people can differ in how they absorb and break down substances, how their immune systems respond and how diseases develop.
- **Disease models.** A lab animal may not develop a human disease on its own, so researchers create a version of it, through genetic changes or by inducing it. That version may not match the condition as it occurs in people.
- **Uniform subjects.** Lab animals are often genetically similar and live in controlled conditions. People vary widely in genes, age, diet, other illnesses and medications.
- **Study size and design.** Some animal studies are small, and some are not randomized or blinded, which makes chance findings and bias more likely.

None of this makes animal research useless. It means a positive result in animals is a reason to run studies in people, and it cannot stand in for them.

## What is the difference between a mechanism and an outcome?

A mechanism is how something works biologically: a compound blocks an enzyme, lowers a marker in the blood or shrinks a tumor in a mouse. An outcome is what matters to a person: living longer, avoiding a heart attack, feeling less pain, staying out of the hospital.

Lab and animal studies are good at showing mechanisms. They cannot show outcomes in people. Even a well-understood mechanism does not guarantee a benefit, because the body is complicated and a change that looks helpful in one pathway can cause harm in another. Improving a lab measurement is not the same as improving health.

Closing that gap is the job of studies in people. [Observational studies](/blog/observational-studies-what-they-can-tell-you) can show whether an exposure is associated with an outcome in real populations. A randomized controlled trial can test whether a treatment causes a difference in outcomes. [What a randomized controlled trial is, and why it matters](/blog/what-is-a-randomized-controlled-trial) explains how those trials work.

## How do headlines overstate lab and animal findings?

Watch for these patterns:

- **The species disappears.** "Compound reverses memory loss in mice" becomes "Compound reverses memory loss."
- **Activity becomes treatment.** "Extract kills cancer cells in a dish" becomes "Extract fights cancer."
- **Possibility becomes promise.** Words like "cure" and "breakthrough" appear for findings that have not been tested in a single person.

AI tools can repeat the same slips. An AI answer may state an animal finding as if it applied to people, or cite a mouse study for a claim about human health. The citation is real, but it does not support the claim as written. [How to fact-check a health claim you read online](/blog/how-to-fact-check-a-health-claim-online) walks through tracing a claim back to its original study.

## How can you tell whether a study was done in people?

A few quick checks:

- **Read the abstract.** The methods usually name the subjects: mice, rats, cell lines, or participants and patients.
- **Look for telltale terms.** In vitro, cell line, cultured cells or the name of an animal in the title or abstract signals that the study may not have been in people.
- **Filter your search.** PubMed lets you filter results by species, including a filter for studies in humans. The filter depends on how records are indexed, so the newest papers may not be caught by it yet.
- **Trace the headline.** Find the original study behind a news story before you repeat a finding.

## Where do these studies sit in the evidence?

For questions about what works in people, lab and animal studies sit near the bottom of the usual ordering, below studies in humans. A finding about a mechanism can be strong evidence about the mechanism and weak evidence about patient outcomes. The full ordering is laid out in [what the hierarchy of evidence is](/blog/hierarchy-of-evidence).

If a lab or animal finding in the news seems relevant to your own health, ask your clinician what is known from studies in people.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do animal studies prove a treatment works in humans?

No. Animal studies can show that a treatment has an effect in that species, but whether it works and is safe in people has to be tested in human studies.

### Why are animal and lab studies done if the results may not apply to people?

They show how biological processes work, help identify which ideas are worth testing in people, and can flag safety problems early. They are a starting point for human research.

### What does in vitro mean?

In vitro means a study was done on cells, tissues or molecules outside a living body, such as in a dish or a test tube. Results from in vitro studies show what happens in that setting, which may differ from what happens in a person.

### How can I tell if a health headline is based on an animal study?

Find the original study and read its abstract, which usually names the subjects, such as mice, rats or cell lines. If the headline leaves out the species, the finding may not have been tested in people.
