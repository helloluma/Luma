A clinical practice guideline is a set of recommendations meant to help clinicians and patients make decisions about care for a specific condition or situation. A well-made guideline comes from a panel that systematically reviews the research, rates how certain the evidence is, weighs benefits against harms, and states how strongly it supports each recommendation.

Guidelines sit between individual studies and everyday care. They translate a body of evidence into practical recommendations, which is why writers cite them and clinicians use them.

## Who writes clinical practice guidelines?

Several kinds of groups produce guidelines:

- Professional medical societies, usually for conditions in their own specialty.
- Government health agencies, often for public health topics such as screening and prevention.
- Independent panels and organizations that focus on reviewing evidence.

A guideline panel typically includes clinicians from the relevant specialties and people with expertise in research methods. Some panels also include patient representatives, so that the recommendations reflect what matters to the people receiving care.

## How is a guideline made?

The details vary by organization, but well-developed guidelines tend to follow a similar sequence.

### 1. Define the questions

The panel sets the scope: which condition, which patients, which decisions. Each question is framed precisely, often by naming the population, the intervention, the comparison, and the outcomes that matter most.

### 2. Review the evidence systematically

Reviewers search the literature, including databases such as PubMed, using a method set out in advance. They select studies against predefined criteria and summarize what those studies found. This systematic review is the foundation of the guideline. For the difference between that and a pooled statistical analysis, see [systematic review versus meta-analysis](/blog/systematic-review-vs-meta-analysis).

### 3. Rate the certainty of the evidence

Evidence varies in how much it can be trusted. Panels rate their confidence in the findings for each outcome, looking at study design, risk of bias, consistency across studies, how directly the studies apply to the question, and how precise the results are.

Randomized controlled trials usually start with higher certainty than observational studies, although either can be rated up or down. The general ordering is explained in [what the hierarchy of evidence is](/blog/hierarchy-of-evidence).

### 4. Weigh benefits, harms and values

Evidence alone does not decide a recommendation. The panel weighs the size of the benefits against the harms and burdens, and considers what patients value. Some panels also consider cost and practicality.

### 5. Write graded recommendations

Each recommendation states what to do and how strongly the panel supports it. A strong recommendation means the panel is confident that the benefits clearly outweigh the harms for most people in the group. A weaker or conditional recommendation means the balance is closer, and the right choice may depend on the individual patient.

### 6. Manage conflicts of interest

Panel members disclose financial and professional interests. Well-run processes limit the role of members whose interests are relevant to a recommendation. How to read those disclosures is covered in [conflicts of interest in medical research](/blog/conflicts-of-interest-medical-research).

### 7. Review and publish

Drafts often go through external review by other experts, and some organizations open drafts for public comment before the final version is published.

## Why do guidelines get updated?

Guidelines change because evidence changes. New trials are published, older findings are refined or overturned, new treatments become available, and new safety information emerges. Some organizations revisit their guidelines on a schedule, or sooner when important new evidence appears.

For readers, **the date and version of a guideline matter as much as its content**. A recommendation from an older version may no longer be current. The same holds for individual studies, as explained in [why the date of a study matters](/blog/why-the-date-of-a-study-matters).

## Why can two guidelines disagree?

Different panels may ask slightly different questions, review the evidence at different times, weigh harms and values differently, or write for different populations and health systems.

Disagreement does not automatically mean one guideline is wrong. It often means the evidence leaves room for judgment. When guidelines differ, compare their questions, their dates, and the strength of the recommendations in question.

## How is a guideline different from a single study?

A single study answers one question in one group of people. A guideline draws on many studies, judges how much they can be trusted, and adds the panel's assessment of benefits, harms and values.

That makes a guideline more useful for practical decisions, but it also means a guideline involves judgment that a single study does not. The strength of each recommendation tells you how much of that judgment was needed.

## What should writers check when citing a guideline?

- Cite the current version and note its date.
- Name the organization that issued it.
- Report the strength of the recommendation along with the recommendation itself.
- Keep a conditional recommendation conditional. Do not turn it into a firm rule.
- Check that the population in the guideline matches the one in your sentence.

PubMed lets you filter search results by article type, including guidelines, which helps you find guidelines published in journals. Some guidelines are published only on the issuing organization's own site, so check there as well.

AI tools sometimes summarize guidelines. Those summaries can drop the strength of a recommendation, the population it applies to, or the date. Check any such summary against the guideline itself before you repeat it.

## What should patients know about guidelines?

Guidelines describe what tends to work for groups of people with a condition. Your clinician applies them to you, taking into account your history, your other conditions, your preferences and your circumstances.

A guideline is a starting point for that conversation and does not replace it. If you read a recommendation and want to know whether it applies to you, bring it to your clinician and ask.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a clinical practice guideline?

A clinical practice guideline is a set of recommendations for care in a specific condition or situation. It is developed by systematically reviewing the research, rating the certainty of the evidence, and weighing benefits against harms.

### Who writes clinical practice guidelines?

Professional medical societies, government health agencies and independent expert panels write them. Panels typically include clinicians and research methods experts, and some include patient representatives.

### Why do clinical practice guidelines change?

They change when new evidence appears, such as new trials, new safety information or new treatments. Checking the date and version of a guideline tells you if its recommendations are current.

### Should I follow a guideline without talking to my doctor?

Guidelines are written for groups of patients, and your clinician applies them to your own situation. Talk with your clinician about how any recommendation applies to you.
