Use an AI drafting tool for structure and wording, and **treat every fact, number and reference it produces as unverified** until you have checked it against the source yourself. The checklist below turns that rule into steps you can run on every draft, each with one line on why it is there.

The items are grouped by stage, so you can work through them in order.

## Why do medical writers need a checklist for AI drafts?

Drafting tools produce fluent, confident prose whether the content is right or wrong. In medical writing, an invented reference or an overstated finding can travel from your draft into a manuscript, a slide deck or a patient handout, and every reviewer after you may assume it was checked.

References are where the risk is easiest to see. In a 2023 study of literature reviews written by an earlier version and a newer version of a widely used chatbot, 55 percent of the citations from the earlier version and 18 percent from the newer version were fabricated (Walters and Wilder, Scientific Reports, 2023). Among the citations that were real, 43 percent of the earlier version's and 24 percent of the newer version's contained substantive errors.

The study covered 42 topics from many fields, not medicine alone, but the lesson carries over.

## What should you set up before you start drafting?

- Read your organization's policy on AI drafting tools, and the client's or journal's rules on disclosing AI use. Why: you need to know what is allowed, and what you must declare, before the draft exists.
- Keep confidential, unpublished or identifiable information out of any tool your organization has not approved. Why: what you type into a drafting tool may be stored outside your control.
- Gather your sources first: the papers, the prescribing information, the protocol, the brief. Why: a draft built from documents you hold can be checked line by line against them.
- Write down the tool's job for this project, such as outlining, restructuring, shortening or simplifying. Why: a tool asked to write about a topic will supply its own facts, and each one becomes something else to verify.

## What should you watch for while drafting?

- Give the tool your source text, not only the topic. Why: output based on a passage you supplied can be compared with that passage.
- Never ask the tool to suggest references from memory. Why: a model writing from memory can produce references that look real and do not exist, and [it cannot tell you which ones it made up](/blog/why-ai-cannot-tell-which-references-are-made-up).
- Highlight every factual sentence the tool adds that did not come from your sources. Why: those sentences have no source yet, so they need one or they come out.
- Watch for hedges that turn into certainty. Why: "may reduce" in a paper becoming "reduces" in your draft changes what the evidence is said to show.
- Copy numbers in from the source rather than letting the tool restate them, and recheck them afterward. Why: numbers shift easily during rephrasing, and a single changed figure can be the whole error.
- Keep track of which passages the tool wrote. Why: if an error surfaces later, or you have to disclose AI use, you will know where to look.

## How do you verify references and claims after drafting?

- Resolve every identifier. Look up each PubMed ID (PMID) on [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and each digital object identifier (DOI) through [doi.org](https://www.doi.org/). Why: an identifier that leads nowhere, or to a different paper, is the fastest sign of a fabricated reference.
- Match the details: authors, title, journal, year, volume and pages. Why: real references can carry wrong details that send readers to the wrong place.
- Read the abstract against the sentence the reference supports, and go to the full text when the claim depends on detail. Why: a real paper can still fail to say what your sentence says.
- Check population, intervention, comparison and outcome. Why: a finding in a different group, or in animals or cells, does not automatically carry over to your claim.
- Check the study design against the strength of your wording. Why: an observational association should not be written up as cause and effect.
- Check for retractions and corrections, as described in our guide on [how to check whether a paper has been retracted](/blog/how-to-check-if-a-paper-was-retracted). Why: PubMed shows a retraction notice on the record of a retracted paper, and drafting tools may still cite retracted work.
- Check the date and look for newer reviews. Why: a finding can be replaced by later evidence or an updated guideline.
- Remove or flag any claim you cannot support. Why: a sentence without a source should never borrow a citation from a nearby paper.

## What should you record before sign-off?

- Keep a verification log with each claim, its reference, the identifier, what you checked, the outcome and the date. Why: a reviewer can repeat your check instead of taking it on trust.
- Ask a second person to check the references, and all of them for high-stakes documents. Why: the writer knows what a sentence was meant to say, while a fresh reader only sees what it does say.
- Get clinical or scientific sign-off on every claim about benefit, harm or safety. Why: verification shows that a source says something, and a qualified reviewer decides whether it belongs in this document.
- Write any required AI disclosure from your drafting notes, in the wording the journal or client asks for. Why: notes kept during drafting make the disclosure accurate.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can medical writers use AI drafting tools?

Yes, for tasks such as outlining, restructuring and editing, as long as your organization, client or journal allows it. Every fact and reference in the draft still has to come from a source you have read and checked.

### Should I ask an AI tool to find references for my draft?

Not from memory. A model writing from memory can produce references that look real but do not exist, so find references by searching PubMed or another database yourself, and verify every one you use.

### What is the fastest way to spot a fabricated reference?

Look up its PMID on PubMed or its DOI through doi.org. If the identifier does not resolve, or resolves to a different paper than the one cited, treat the reference as suspect until you find the real source.

### What should a verification log include?

Each claim, the reference that supports it, the reference's identifier, what you checked, the outcome and the date. That record lets anyone repeat the check later.
