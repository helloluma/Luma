Editors and peer reviewers can screen for fabricated references by resolving every identifier in a reference list, matching each record's details against the citation, and spot-checking that the cited papers say what the manuscript claims. The identifier and metadata checks are mechanical and can run at submission, which leaves reviewers free to focus on whether the references support the argument.

The steps below fit into an existing editorial workflow. All of them run on public records.

## How common are fabricated references in published papers?

Common enough to justify a routine screen. An audit by Topaz and colleagues, published in The Lancet in 2026, checked 97.1 million structured references across about 2.5 million biomedical papers. It found 4,046 fabricated references across 2,810 papers.

The rate is rising. Roughly one in 277 papers published in the first seven weeks of 2026 cited a paper that does not exist, up from one in 458 in 2025 and one in 2,828 in 2023. And 98.4 percent of affected papers had received no publisher action at the time of the audit. More on the audit is in [how common fabricated references are in biomedical papers](/blog/how-common-are-fabricated-references-biomedical-papers).

The same audit reports that prior studies estimate 30 to 69 percent of references generated by large language models in biomedical contexts are fabricated. Any manuscript drafted with AI help deserves a careful reference check for that reason alone.

## What does a fabricated reference look like?

A fabricated reference can look entirely ordinary. It may borrow real parts:

- A plausible title on the right topic.
- Author names that belong to real researchers in the field.
- A real journal, often one that publishes on the subject.
- A year, volume and page range that look normal.

What gives it away is that the pieces do not belong together. The identifier is missing, or it resolves to a different paper. The authors are real but never wrote that title. The volume and pages belong to another article.

For the harder cases, see [how to spot a citation that has been subtly altered](/blog/how-to-spot-an-altered-citation).

## What screening steps catch fabricated references?

### Step 1: Resolve every identifier

Look up each digital object identifier (DOI) through the [DOI resolver](https://www.doi.org/) and each PubMed ID (PMID) on [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). A DOI that does not resolve, or a PMID that leads to a different paper, is the clearest single warning sign. Crossref, which registers DOIs for many scholarly publishers, also provides metadata you can match against a citation.

### Step 2: Match the details

For each resolved record, compare the title, authors, journal, year, volume and pages with the citation. Small differences can be honest typos. A record that matches on the identifier but differs in title or authors needs a closer look.

### Step 3: Search references that have no identifier

Search PubMed for the exact title, then for the first author with a few distinctive words from the title. Books, reports and some older papers may not appear there and need a library catalog or the publisher's site. A biomedical journal article that cannot be found anywhere is a serious warning sign.

### Step 4: Check retraction status

PubMed shows retraction notices on retracted records. A manuscript that leans on retracted work has a different problem from fabrication, but it belongs in the same screen. [How to check whether a paper has been retracted](/blog/how-to-check-if-a-paper-was-retracted) covers the details.

### Step 5: Spot-check support

A real paper can still be cited for something it does not say. Pick the references that carry the manuscript's main claims, read their abstracts and confirm each one supports the sentence it is attached to. Watch for a different population, a different outcome, or a tentative finding cited as settled.

## Who should do which step?

Splitting the work keeps it manageable:

- **The editorial office** runs steps 1 through 4 at submission, before a manuscript goes out for review. These checks are mechanical, and reference-checking software can automate much of them.
- **Reviewers** take step 5 for the references in their area, where their knowledge of the literature helps them notice a paper cited for the wrong finding.
- **The handling editor** decides what to do with anything flagged and makes sure the outcome is recorded.

Reviewers should not be asked to resolve every identifier in a long reference list by hand. Their time is better spent on judgment the software cannot make.

## What should an editor do when a reference fails?

Start with the least serious explanation. Ask the authors to supply the full text of the cited work or a corrected reference. A single wrong page number is most likely a typo.

A pattern is different. Several references to papers that do not exist, or a reference list with many mismatched identifiers, calls for a fuller look under the journal's policies. Record what was checked, what failed and how it was resolved, so the decision can be reviewed later.

For papers already published, the same checks can run after the fact. Given that 98.4 percent of affected papers in the audit had received no publisher action, correction processes for published work deserve attention too. [What happens when fabricated citations enter the published literature](/blog/fabricated-citations-in-published-literature) looks at the downstream harm.

## Should journals ask authors to confirm their references?

A short author attestation helps: a statement that every reference was checked against the original source by a named author. It places responsibility with the people who wrote the paper and tells them the journal is looking. Author guidance can also say plainly that any reference list drafted with AI help must be verified item by item before submission.

Attestation does not replace screening. It sets expectations, and the screen confirms them.

## Frequently asked questions

### How can a reviewer tell whether a reference is fabricated?

Look the reference up by its DOI or PMID and confirm the record matches the citation's title, authors, journal and year. If there is no identifier, search PubMed for the exact title; a biomedical journal article that cannot be found anywhere is a strong sign of fabrication.

### Should every reference be checked, or only a sample?

Check every identifier, because that step is quick and can be automated. Checking whether each cited paper supports its claim takes longer, so reviewers can focus on the references behind the manuscript's main claims.

### Is a fabricated reference always misconduct?

Not necessarily. A single error may be a typo or a copying mistake, so ask the authors first; a pattern of references to papers that do not exist calls for a fuller review under the journal's policies.
