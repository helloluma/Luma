"Statistically significant" means a result would be fairly unlikely to turn up if the thing being tested had no real effect. It does not mean the effect is large, that it matters to patients, or that it is certain to be real.

Much of the confusion comes from the word "significant." In everyday speech it means important. In statistics it means something much narrower.

## What does "statistically significant" mean in plain words?

Researchers start from a working assumption that the treatment or exposure makes no difference. Statisticians call this the null hypothesis. They then ask how surprising their data would be if that assumption were true.

If the data would be quite surprising under "no effect," and the result passes a cutoff the researchers set before the study, they call it statistically significant. In plain terms, chance alone would rarely produce a result this extreme if there were truly no effect.

## What is a p-value?

The p-value is the number behind most claims of significance. It is the probability of getting a result at least as extreme as the one observed, assuming there is truly no effect. A small p-value means the data would be unusual in a world where the treatment does nothing.

Two common misreadings are worth avoiding:

- The p-value is **not** the probability that the finding is true.
- It is also not the probability that the result happened by chance.

It answers a narrower question: if there were no effect, how often would data like these turn up? Researchers compare the p-value with a threshold chosen in advance. Below the threshold, the result is called significant. Above it, the result is called not significant.

## Does statistically significant mean the effect is large?

No. Significance depends on two things: how big the effect is and how much data the study has. A very large study can detect a tiny effect and call it significant.

Take a hypothetical trial with an enormous number of participants, in which a new drug lowers a symptom score by a sliver compared with placebo. The difference could clear the significance threshold and still be too small for any patient to notice. **Statistical significance and clinical significance are separate questions.** Clinical significance asks whether a difference is big enough to matter to patients.

## Does "not significant" mean there is no effect?

No. A result that is not statistically significant means the study did not find strong enough evidence against "no effect." That can happen because there is no effect, or because the study was too small or too short to detect one.

Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. A small study that finds "no significant difference" may simply have lacked the power to see a real one.

## What is a confidence interval, and why does it help?

A confidence interval is a range of effect sizes that are reasonably compatible with the data. It tells you two things a p-value alone does not: roughly how big the effect might be, and how uncertain that estimate is.

A narrow interval means the study pinned the effect down fairly precisely. A wide interval means the true effect could be small or large, and if the interval includes zero difference, possibly nothing at all. When you can, look at the interval and ask whether the values it covers would matter in practice.

## Why can a significant result still be wrong?

Significance is a statement about chance under certain assumptions. It cannot rule out:

- False positives: unusual results happen by chance, and some of them will cross the threshold.
- Many comparisons: a study that tests many outcomes will find some significant ones even if nothing is going on.
- Bias: flaws in how participants were chosen, assigned or measured can produce a real-looking difference that the treatment did not cause.
- Reliance on one study: a single significant result is a first sighting, and [why one study is rarely the final word](/blog/why-one-study-is-rarely-the-final-word) explains why replication matters.

A well-designed [randomized controlled trial](/blog/what-is-a-randomized-controlled-trial) reduces bias, but no design removes chance.

## How should you read "significant" in a news story or AI answer?

Ask which meaning is intended. A headline or an AI-generated summary may say a treatment "significantly reduced" an outcome, and readers hear "reduced by a lot." The paper may mean only that the reduction passed a statistical test.

Look for the size of the effect, in absolute terms where possible. A large relative change in a rare outcome can be a very small absolute change, as explained in [relative risk versus absolute risk](/blog/relative-risk-vs-absolute-risk). Check the study design and the number of participants, and look at whether other studies found the same thing.

AI summaries deserve the same care. They can turn a hedged finding into a firm one, and "significant" is an easy word to carry over without its statistical meaning. When it matters, go back to the abstract, and see [how to read a PubMed abstract in five minutes](/blog/how-to-read-a-pubmed-abstract) for a quick method.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does statistically significant mean the result is true?

No. It means the result would be unlikely if there were no real effect, under the assumptions of the test. Chance, bias and multiple comparisons can still produce significant results that do not hold up.

### What is the difference between statistical and clinical significance?

Statistical significance asks whether a result would be unlikely if there were no real effect. Clinical significance asks whether the size of the effect would make a real difference to patients, and a result can have one without the other.

### Why do large studies find significant results more easily?

More data makes estimates more precise, so smaller differences can pass the significance threshold. That precision is useful, but it also means a significant result from a very large study can be too small to matter.

### What should I look at besides the p-value?

Look at the size of the effect, its confidence interval, the study design and the number of participants. Then check whether other studies have found the same thing.
