A systematic review is a structured search for, and appraisal of, all the studies that address a specific question, carried out by a method set out in advance. A meta-analysis is a statistical technique that combines the numerical results of several studies into one estimate, and it is often done as one step within a systematic review.

Put simply, the systematic review gathers and judges the evidence, and the meta-analysis does the arithmetic on it. A review can exist without a meta-analysis, and a meta-analysis is only as trustworthy as the process that chose its studies.

## What is a systematic review?

A systematic review answers a focused question, such as whether a treatment improves a specific outcome in a specific group of people. What makes it systematic is that every step follows a plan written before the work begins:

1. Define the question, including the population, intervention, comparison and outcome.
2. Write a protocol that sets out the methods in advance.
3. Search several databases, such as PubMed, along with other sources, for every potentially relevant study.
4. Screen the results against inclusion and exclusion criteria fixed in the protocol, ideally with more than one reviewer working independently.
5. Assess each included study's risk of bias.
6. Extract the data and synthesize the findings.

The synthesis can be a written comparison of the studies, called a narrative synthesis, or it can include a meta-analysis.

**The plan is what separates a systematic review from an ordinary literature review.** In a narrative literature review, the author chooses which papers to discuss. In a systematic review, the protocol decides, which makes it harder to leave out inconvenient studies and easier for others to repeat the search.

## What is a meta-analysis?

A meta-analysis takes the results of several studies that measured the same outcome and combines them statistically. Larger and more precise studies usually get more weight. The output is a pooled estimate of the effect, with a range that reflects the uncertainty around it.

Results are often shown in a forest plot: each study appears as its own line showing its result and uncertainty, with the pooled result at the bottom.

A meta-analysis also looks at heterogeneity, meaning how much the study results differ from one another. If the studies disagree a lot, a single pooled number can hide real differences between populations, treatments or methods.

Pooling only makes sense when the studies are similar enough. Combining trials of different drugs or different outcomes into one number produces a precise-looking answer to a question nobody asked.

## How do systematic reviews and meta-analyses relate?

There are three combinations you will see:

- A systematic review without a meta-analysis. The studies are too different to pool, so the review compares them in words.
- A systematic review with a meta-analysis. The review gathers and appraises the studies, and the meta-analysis pools the ones similar enough to combine.
- A meta-analysis without a systematic review. Someone pools a set of studies without a full systematic search. Treat these with extra care, because the choice of studies can drive the result.

When a title says "a systematic review and meta-analysis", it usually describes the second case.

## Why do they sit near the top of the hierarchy of evidence?

A single study can produce a misleading result by chance, through bias, or because of who happened to enroll. Looking at the full body of evidence reduces the influence of any one study. That is why systematic reviews and meta-analyses usually sit at the top of the [hierarchy of evidence](/blog/hierarchy-of-evidence), and why [one study is rarely the final word](/blog/why-one-study-is-rarely-the-final-word).

## What can a systematic review or meta-analysis not tell you?

- A review is only as good as the studies it includes. Pooling several weak or biased studies gives a precise-looking estimate of a biased answer.
- Missing studies skew the picture. If studies that found nothing were never published, even a thorough search cannot find them, and the pooled result will look stronger than the truth. The problem is called publication bias.
- Different studies may not be comparable. High heterogeneity can make a single pooled number misleading.
- Reviews age. A review reflects the studies available when its search was run, and newer trials may change the picture. See [why the date of a study matters](/blog/why-the-date-of-a-study-matters).
- The question may not be yours. A review of one population or outcome may say little about another.
- Reviews on the same question can disagree, depending on how each one chose and judged its studies.

## How do you find systematic reviews and meta-analyses in PubMed?

PubMed, the free search engine for biomedical literature maintained by the National Library of Medicine, lets you filter results by article type, and the options include systematic reviews and meta-analyses. Combining your topic terms with that filter narrows a search to reviews quickly. Our guide on [how to search PubMed for evidence on a specific claim](/blog/how-to-search-pubmed-for-a-specific-claim) covers the search side.

Check the methods before you trust the label. A genuine systematic review describes its search, the databases it used, its inclusion criteria and how it assessed bias. If those details are missing, it may be a narrative review with a systematic-sounding title.

## How should writers describe them accurately?

- Call a review systematic only if its methods show it.
- Report the pooled finding with the review's own caveats, including how consistent the studies were and how strong the authors judged the evidence.
- Name the population and outcome the review covered.
- Check the article type on the PubMed record instead of trusting a summary's label. AI-generated summaries can describe a narrative review as a meta-analysis, or drop the authors' warnings about weak evidence.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a meta-analysis a type of systematic review?

Not exactly. A meta-analysis is a statistical method for pooling results, and it is often done as one part of a systematic review. A systematic review can be published without any meta-analysis.

### Can you have a systematic review without a meta-analysis?

Yes. When the included studies are too different to combine, the review compares them in words instead, which is called a narrative synthesis.

### Which is stronger, a systematic review or a meta-analysis?

Neither is automatically stronger. A systematic review that pools well-matched, high-quality studies in a meta-analysis gives a strong summary, while a meta-analysis of a hand-picked or poorly matched set of studies can mislead.

### What is the difference between a literature review and a systematic review?

In a narrative literature review, the author decides which studies to discuss. A systematic review follows a protocol set in advance for searching, selecting and appraising studies, so others can check and repeat it.
