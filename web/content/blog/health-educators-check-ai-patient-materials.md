Check AI-drafted patient materials the way you would check a draft from a new writer whose sources you have not seen: list every factual claim, confirm each one against a reliable source, then review reading level, tone and completeness before a clinician signs off. AI can save drafting time, but it cannot be the source of the facts in a handout.

The steps below work for handouts, web pages, discharge sheets and class materials.

## Why do AI-drafted materials need a different review?

AI tools write in a clear, confident, even style. That makes their drafts easy to read and hard to review, because a wrong claim looks exactly as polished as a correct one. The problem is explained further in [why fluent writing makes AI medical errors harder to spot](/blog/why-fluent-ai-writing-hides-errors).

AI tools can also produce references that do not exist. In a 2023 study by Walters and Wilder of literature reviews written by an earlier version and a newer version of a widely used chatbot, 55 percent of the citations from the earlier version and 18 percent from the newer version were fabricated. That study covered many fields, not only medicine, but the lesson carries over. Treat every reference in an AI draft as a lead to check.

Patient materials also reach people who may act on them with no clinician in the room. That raises the bar for every sentence.

## What is the review process, step by step?

### 1. List every factual claim

Go through the draft and pull out each statement that could be true or false. "This medicine can cause a dry cough" is a claim. So is any statement about how common something is, how long something lasts, or what a test shows.

Keep the list in a separate document or in a column next to the draft. Splitting the text this way is the basis of [claim-level verification](/blog/what-is-claim-level-verification), and it stops one wrong sentence from hiding inside a mostly correct page.

Instructions such as when to call the care team are a separate category. Those should come from your organization's clinical policies, not from the AI draft.

### 2. Check each claim against a source

For each claim, find a reliable source: your organization's approved clinical content, a current clinical practice guideline, or published research on PubMed. Note which source supports which claim.

If the draft came with references, confirm that each one exists. Look it up by its PubMed ID (PMID) or digital object identifier (DOI), match the title, authors and journal, and read the abstract to confirm it says what the draft says. The full procedure is in [how to check if a PubMed citation is real](/blog/how-to-check-if-a-pubmed-citation-is-real).

**Any claim you cannot support gets rewritten or removed.** Do not keep it because it sounds right.

### 3. Look for what is missing

An AI draft can be accurate and still incomplete. Compare it with your organization's approved content on the same topic. Check that it covers what your organization requires, such as warning signs and how to reach the care team.

Also look for claims stated more strongly than the evidence allows. Words like "always," "never," "proven" and "guaranteed" deserve a second look.

### 4. Check reading level and plain language

Read the draft as a patient would. Look for:

- Medical terms with no explanation.
- Long sentences that carry several ideas.
- Numbers presented in confusing ways, such as a relative change in risk with no baseline.
- Instructions buried in paragraphs instead of placed where a reader will find them.

Readability formulas estimate difficulty from sentence length and word length. They are a quick screen, but they cannot tell you whether a reader will understand the content. Testing a draft with patients or community reviewers, where your organization does that, tells you more.

### 5. Check tone, culture and language

AI drafts can carry assumptions about diet, family structure, access to care or cost that do not fit your audience. Check examples and suggestions against the communities you serve.

If the material will be translated, have a qualified translator or bilingual reviewer check the translated version as carefully as the original.

### 6. Get clinician sign-off

A clinician with the right expertise should review the final text for accuracy and safety and approve it before publication. Give the reviewer the claim list and the sources, along with the draft. A reviewer with sources in hand is checking the facts instead of only reading for tone.

### 7. Record the review

Keep a record with the final version: the date, the reviewers, the source for each claim, and whether AI was used in drafting. Set a date to review the material again, since evidence and guidance change. For the wider policy questions, see [how health teams can bring AI assistants into their work responsibly](/blog/health-teams-ai-assistants-responsibly).

## What are the red flags in an AI-drafted handout?

- Specific numbers with no source attached.
- References you cannot find on PubMed or at the journal.
- Absolute language about benefits or risks.
- Guidance that should come from a patient's own clinician, written as a general instruction.
- Content that contradicts your organization's existing materials.
- A smooth, confident draft on a topic where the evidence is unsettled.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can AI write patient education materials?

AI can produce a useful first draft. Every factual claim in it still needs to be checked against a reliable source, and the final text needs clinician sign-off before patients see it.

### How do I check the references in an AI-drafted handout?

Look up each reference by its PubMed ID or digital object identifier, confirm that the title, authors and journal match, and read the abstract to confirm it supports the claim. Remove any reference you cannot find.

### Who should approve AI-generated patient materials?

A clinician with the right expertise should approve the final text through your organization's normal review process. Using AI to draft should not shorten that process.

### Is a readability score enough to check plain language?

No. Readability formulas estimate difficulty from sentence and word length, so they work as a quick screen, but testing the material with real readers tells you whether it is understood.
