The date of a study matters because medical evidence accumulates and changes: later studies can confirm an older finding, narrow it, or overturn it, and guidelines are revised to match. Before you rely on a study, check when it was published and whether newer research or a recent review has addressed the same question.

An older study is not automatically wrong. Many foundational findings, such as the link between smoking and lung cancer, have held up as later research piled on. The date tells you what else to look for.

## Why does newer evidence change older conclusions?

Several things can change between an older study and today.

- **More studies.** A single early study may be followed by larger ones, and the combined body of evidence can point somewhere the first result did not. There is more on this in [why one study is rarely the final word](/blog/why-one-study-is-rarely-the-final-word).
- **Better comparisons.** A treatment tested against a placebo may later be compared with a newer treatment that did not exist when the first study ran.
- **Changed definitions.** The cutoffs used to define some conditions, such as high blood pressure, have been revised over the years. A study that used an older definition counted a different group of people as having the condition.
- **Better methods.** Study design, measurement tools and reporting standards improve over time, which can make old results hard to compare with new ones.
- **Different populations.** The people studied years ago may differ from patients today in age, other health conditions, or the treatments they were already taking.

None of these make an old study useless. They mean an old study answers the question as it stood at the time it was done.

## How do guidelines change over time?

Clinical practice guidelines summarize the evidence on a question and turn it into recommendations. They are reviewed and updated as new evidence appears, so a recommendation that was standard when one version was published may be changed in the next. [How a clinical practice guideline is made](/blog/what-is-a-clinical-practice-guideline) explains who writes them and how evidence is weighed.

When you read a guideline, check its publication or update date and look for a newer version. A news story or website quoting a guideline may be quoting one that has since been replaced.

## Can a study change after it is published?

A published paper can later receive a correction, an expression of concern, or a retraction. PubMed displays retraction notices on the records of retracted papers, so checking the current record, and not only a copy you saved years ago, is worth the extra minute. The steps are in [how to check whether a paper has been retracted](/blog/how-to-check-if-a-paper-was-retracted).

Corrections matter too. A correction can change a number in a table or a sentence in the conclusion, and anyone citing the original version may be repeating something the authors have since fixed.

## Why do AI tools sometimes cite older evidence?

A large language model learns from text collected up to a training cutoff. Anything published after that point is unknown to the model unless the system around it retrieves new material. Even for older material, the model blends many sources, and it has no built-in way to tell you which of them is the most recent.

As a result, an AI answer can sound current while resting on evidence that later work has refined. Systems that search the literature at the moment you ask, instead of relying on memory, have a better chance of finding newer studies. The trade-off is covered in [why live literature retrieval beats a model's memory](/blog/live-literature-retrieval-vs-model-memory).

## How do you check whether a study is still current?

These habits work for writers, students and patients reading about their own health.

1. **Note the publication date.** It appears on the PubMed record and on the paper itself.
2. **Search for newer work on the same question.** PubMed lets you filter results by publication date and sort them so the most recent appear first.
3. **Look for a recent systematic review.** A systematic review gathers the studies on a question and judges them together. Filtering PubMed by article type helps you find one.
4. **Check for a current guideline.** If a guideline covers the question, compare its date and its recommendation with the study you are reading.
5. **Check the record for notices.** Look for corrections or retraction notices linked to the paper.
6. **Ask a clinician about anything that affects your own care.** They can tell you whether a study you read applies to you.

## Does newer always mean better?

No. A recent study can be small, early or poorly designed, and an older randomized controlled trial may still be the best evidence available on a question. The date is one factor alongside study design, size, and how well the results agree with other work.

The useful question is whether the newest high-quality evidence agrees with the study in front of you. If it does, the older study still stands. If it does not, find out why before you rely on the older one.

Recent studies on a fast-moving topic also deserve some patience. A first result that has not yet been repeated by other groups is a reason to watch for follow-up work, and a reason to be careful about repeating it as settled.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are older medical studies still reliable?
Many are, especially when later research has confirmed them. Check whether newer studies, a recent systematic review or a current guideline address the same question before relying on an older result.

### How do I find newer research on the same topic?
Search PubMed for the same question, filter by publication date and sort by most recent. Filtering by article type can help you find a recent systematic review.

### Why do medical guidelines change?
Guidelines are reviewed and updated as new evidence appears. A recommendation can change when newer studies show a different balance of benefits and harms.

### Can an AI tool tell me if a study is out of date?
Not reliably on its own. A model writing from memory has a training cutoff and may not know about newer work, so check the current literature or ask a clinician.
