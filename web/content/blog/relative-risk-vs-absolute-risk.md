Absolute risk is the chance that something happens to a group of people over a stated period of time. Relative risk compares the absolute risks of two groups, so it tells you how much bigger or smaller the risk is in one group than in the other, without telling you how big the risk was to begin with.

Both are correct ways to describe the same result. They can leave very different impressions, which is why a health claim that reports only one of them is incomplete.

## What is absolute risk?

Absolute risk is the plain probability of an outcome in a group over a time period. When you read that an outcome happens to a certain number of people out of every hundred over a stated number of years, that is an absolute risk.

It answers the practical question: how likely is this outcome for people like the ones studied?

## What is relative risk?

Relative risk divides the risk in one group by the risk in another. Studies usually report it when they compare a treated group with a control group, or an exposed group with an unexposed group.

When the two risks are equal, the relative risk is one, meaning no difference. Below one, the outcome was less common in the first group. Above one, it was more common.

A related figure, the relative risk reduction, expresses the drop in risk as a share of the comparison group's risk. For the same result, it always sounds larger than the absolute change.

## How do the two numbers compare in a worked example?

Every number in this section is hypothetical. None of it describes a real treatment, condition or study.

Take a hypothetical trial of a hypothetical drug meant to prevent a hypothetical bad outcome over the study period:

- In this hypothetical trial, 2 out of every 100 people in the control group have the outcome. Their hypothetical absolute risk is 2 percent.
- In the same hypothetical trial, 1 out of every 100 people taking the drug has the outcome. Their hypothetical absolute risk is 1 percent.

Now calculate both measures for this hypothetical result:

- Relative risk: in this hypothetical example, 1 percent divided by 2 percent is 0.5. The hypothetical relative risk reduction is therefore 50 percent.
- Absolute risk reduction: in this hypothetical example, 2 percent minus 1 percent is 1 percentage point.

For this hypothetical trial, "cuts the risk in half" and "lowers the risk by 1 percentage point" are both true. **They describe the same result and feel completely different.**

## What is the number needed to treat?

The number needed to treat turns the absolute risk reduction into a count of people. It equals one divided by the absolute risk reduction.

In the hypothetical example above, the absolute risk reduction is 1 percentage point, so the hypothetical number needed to treat is 100. In that hypothetical scenario, 100 people would take the drug over the study period for one of them to avoid the outcome. The other hypothetical 99 people get no benefit on that outcome, although they are still exposed to any side effects.

The number needed to treat makes trade-offs concrete, and you can calculate it yourself when a paper reports the event counts in each group.

## Why does baseline risk change the picture?

The same relative reduction means very different things depending on how common the outcome was to begin with. Here are two more hypothetical cases, each with a hypothetical 50 percent relative risk reduction:

- In a hypothetical high-risk group with a hypothetical baseline risk of 20 in 100, halving the risk prevents 10 outcomes per 100 people, for a hypothetical number needed to treat of 10.
- In a hypothetical low-risk group with a hypothetical baseline risk of 2 in 1,000, halving the risk prevents 1 outcome per 1,000 people, for a hypothetical number needed to treat of 1,000.

The relative figure is identical in both hypothetical cases. The absolute benefit differs by a factor of a hundred in these hypothetical numbers. That is why the question of whether a result matters for a particular person depends on that person's own baseline risk, and why that conversation belongs with their clinician.

## Does the same problem apply to harms?

Yes. A side effect that "doubles the risk" sounds alarming. If a hypothetical baseline risk of 1 in 10,000 doubles, the hypothetical absolute risk becomes 2 in 10,000, an increase of one person in ten thousand in this hypothetical case.

A doubled risk can still matter. You just cannot judge it without the baseline.

## What about odds ratios and hazard ratios?

Papers also report odds ratios and hazard ratios. These are related relative measures with their own definitions, and they share the same limitation: on their own, they do not tell you how common the outcome was in either group.

## How should you read risk numbers in a study or a headline?

1. Find the baseline. What was the risk in the comparison group?
2. Look for both numbers. If only a relative change is given, look for the event counts in each group in the abstract or the results tables.
3. Check the time period. Risks measured over different periods are not comparable.
4. Check who was studied. Baseline risk depends on the population.
5. Look at the outcome. A change in a lab measurement is different from a change in hospital admissions or deaths.
6. Apply the same steps to harms. Side effects need their baselines too.

Two more checks help. A result can be [statistically significant](/blog/what-statistically-significant-means) and still small in absolute terms. And a relative risk from an [observational study](/blog/observational-studies-what-they-can-tell-you) shows an association, which may or may not reflect cause and effect.

## What should writers and AI answers do?

If you write about research, report the absolute risks in each group alongside any relative figure, and state the time period and the population. Where you can, give the number needed to treat.

Apply the same standard to AI-generated summaries. An answer that says a treatment "reduces risk by half" may be accurate and still mislead if it leaves out the baseline. Check the claim against the paper's results, using the steps in [how to tell whether a study actually supports a claim](/blog/how-to-tell-if-a-study-supports-a-claim).

If you are reading about a treatment you are considering, bring the numbers to your clinician. They can explain what a result means for someone with your own history and baseline risk.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between relative risk and absolute risk?

Absolute risk is the chance of an outcome in a group over a set period of time. Relative risk compares the absolute risks of two groups, so it shows the size of a change without showing how common the outcome was.

### Why can relative risk sound bigger than absolute risk?

When an outcome is uncommon, a large relative change can correspond to a very small absolute change. Reporting only the relative figure leaves out how many people are actually affected.

### What is the number needed to treat?

It is the number of people who would need to receive a treatment over a stated period for one of them to avoid the outcome. It equals one divided by the absolute risk reduction.

### Should I make a health decision based on a relative risk number?

Bring any risk numbers you read to your clinician rather than acting on them alone. Your clinician can explain what a result means for your own baseline risk and situation.
