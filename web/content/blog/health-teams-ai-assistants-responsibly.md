Health teams can bring AI assistants into their work responsibly by putting three things in place before the first AI-assisted document goes out: a written policy on what the assistant may be used for, a rule that every factual claim carries a source a person has checked, and a named person who signs off. The assistant can speed up drafting, while responsibility for what goes out stays with people.

Most of the risk sits in one place. AI assistants write fluent, confident text, and fluent text can contain claims that are wrong or references that do not exist. A team that plans for that from the start can use the tools for what they do well without passing their errors on to patients, partners or the public.

## Why do health teams need a policy before using AI assistants?

People will use the tools either way. Without a policy, each person sets their own rules: one staff member checks every reference, another trusts whatever looks plausible, and nobody can say afterward which documents were checked.

The cost of an unchecked reference is real. An audit by Topaz and colleagues, published in The Lancet in 2026, checked 97.1 million structured references across about 2.5 million biomedical papers. It found that roughly one in 277 papers published in the first seven weeks of 2026 cited a paper that does not exist, up from one in 458 in 2025 and one in 2,828 in 2023. At the time of the audit, 98.4 percent of affected papers had received no publisher action.

If fabricated references can reach peer-reviewed journals, they can reach a patient handout just as easily. Fluency is part of the problem, as covered in [why fluent writing makes AI medical errors harder to spot](/blog/why-fluent-ai-writing-hides-errors).

## What should an AI use policy cover?

Keep it short enough that people read it. A one-page policy that staff follow does more than a long one nobody opens.

- Approved uses: drafting, summarizing documents you supply, rewriting text at a plainer reading level, reformatting, and brainstorming search terms.
- Uses that need extra review: anything patient-facing, anything that states a clinical fact, and anything that will carry your organization's name.
- Uses that are off limits: treating the assistant as the source of a fact, a reference or a clinical decision.
- Data rules: which tools are approved, and what information may be typed into each. Your organization's existing privacy obligations apply to anything pasted into an assistant, so involve your privacy or compliance lead.
- Records: keep the prompt, the raw output and the reviewed version of anything that goes out.

Name an owner for the policy and set a time to revisit it. Tools change, and so do the ways people use them.

## What source requirements should every AI-assisted document meet?

Set one standard and apply it to every document that states a fact: **every factual claim must trace to a source a person has opened and read.** A reference the assistant supplied does not count until someone has confirmed that it exists and that it says what the sentence says.

In practice, that means:

1. Each reference resolves. A PubMed ID (PMID) or digital object identifier (DOI) leads to a real record whose title, authors, journal and year match the citation.
2. The source supports the specific claim, for the same population and the same outcome. A real paper can still be the wrong paper, as explained in [why a real citation can still be the wrong citation](/blog/real-citation-wrong-claim).
3. Claims without support are removed, narrowed to what the evidence does show, or sent to a subject expert. They never get a citation added just to look credible.
4. The check is recorded, so a reviewer can see which claims were verified and how.

For a full procedure, see [how to verify an AI-generated reference list, step by step](/blog/verify-ai-generated-reference-list).

## What does a practical review step look like?

Split the work so that the person who drafted a document is never its only checker. A workable sequence:

1. Draft: the author uses the assistant within the policy and marks every factual claim in the text.
2. Source check: a second person, or a verification tool followed by a person, confirms each claim against its source and records it as supported, partly supported, or not supported.
3. Expert review: a clinician or subject expert reads the claims that carry clinical weight and judges whether the document is right for its audience.
4. Sign-off: one named person approves it.

Checking claim by claim keeps the work focused. A mostly right document can hide one wrong sentence, and reading the whole thing at once tends to miss it. [Claim-level verification](/blog/what-is-claim-level-verification) is the habit of checking each sentence on its own.

## Who should sign off on AI-assisted health content?

One named person per document, with the authority to approve it and the expertise to judge it. For clinical content, that is a clinician on the team. For patient education, it is a clinician plus whoever owns reading level and format. For internal summaries, it is the manager who will act on them.

The assistant never signs off, and neither does any other software. Tools can narrow the work by showing which claims have support and which do not. A person decides what to publish.

Write the approver's name on the record. When a question comes up months later, the team should be able to say who approved the document and what was checked.

## How do you keep review from slowing everyone down?

Match the depth of review to the risk. A patient handout about a medicine deserves a full source check and clinical review. An internal meeting summary drawn from your own notes needs a quick read by the person who ran the meeting.

## What should happen when an error gets through?

Treat it like any other quality problem. Correct the document, follow your usual process for telling the people who received it, and record which step failed: the policy, the source check or the review. Then fix the step that missed it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can health teams use AI assistants for patient-facing materials?

Yes, if the material goes through the same source check and clinical review as anything else a patient will read. The assistant can draft and simplify, and a clinician should approve the final version.

### Should AI-generated references ever be used without checking?

No. An AI assistant can produce references that look real but do not exist, or real references that do not support the sentence they are attached to, so each one needs to be opened and read before use.

### Who is responsible when an AI-assisted document contains an error?

The organization and the person who approved the document, the same as for any other document. Every AI-assisted document should carry the name of the person who signed off for exactly this reason.

### How often should a team review its AI use policy?

Review it whenever the approved tools change, after any error that reached an audience, and on a regular schedule set by the policy owner. Small, frequent updates keep the policy matched to how people actually work.
