Live retrieval wins because it reads the current published record at the moment a question is asked, while a model's memory is a fixed, compressed blend of whatever text it saw during training. For medical facts, that difference decides whether you can point to the exact paper behind a claim, and whether that paper still stands.

## What does a model actually remember?

A large language model learns patterns from a very large amount of text during training. It does not keep a shelf of documents with their titles and identifiers attached. When it answers from memory, it generates text that fits the patterns it learned.

For facts repeated many times in its training text, that often produces the right words. For specifics, such as which paper reported a finding, who wrote it, the year, the journal or the PubMed ID (PMID), the model has to reconstruct details that were never stored as a unit. The pieces can come from different sources and blend into a reference that looks real and points nowhere. Our post on [why AI models cannot tell you which of their references they made up](/blog/why-ai-cannot-tell-which-references-are-made-up) explains why asking the model to double-check itself does not fix this.

The problem has been measured. An audit of references in biomedical papers by Topaz and colleagues, published in The Lancet in 2026, reports that prior studies estimate 30 to 69 percent of references generated by large language models in biomedical contexts are fabricated.

## Why does a training cutoff matter in medicine?

A model's knowledge stops at its training cutoff, the point after which no new text went into training. Medicine keeps moving after that point. New trials are published, guidelines are revised, safety warnings are issued, and papers are corrected or retracted.

A model answering from memory cannot know about any of it. An answer based on a finding that was retracted after the cutoff can read exactly like an answer based on a finding that still holds. Our post on [why the date of a study matters](/blog/why-the-date-of-a-study-matters) explains how newer evidence can replace older findings.

## What does live retrieval do differently?

Live retrieval searches a literature database at answer time and works from the records it gets back. For biomedical questions, the natural source is PubMed, the free search engine for biomedical literature maintained by the National Library of Medicine at the National Institutes of Health.

Three things change when the answer starts from retrieved records:

1. Every source is a real record with a stable identifier, such as a PMID, that anyone can open and read.
2. The record is current as of the search. If a paper has been retracted, its PubMed record carries a retraction notice that the system can see and act on.
3. The same question asked next month is checked against next month's literature, without retraining anything.

## Does retrieval solve the problem on its own?

It narrows the problem. Retrieval-augmented generation, where a model writes its answer from documents fetched for the question, gives the model real records to cite. A model can still misstate what a retrieved paper says, attach a real paper to a claim it does not support, or mix in details from its own memory. We cover this in [what retrieval-augmented generation is, and whether it stops hallucinations](/blog/does-retrieval-augmented-generation-stop-hallucinations).

Retrieval also brings its own failure modes. A poorly built search can miss the relevant papers, and an empty result can look like an absence of evidence when it is only an absence of matches. An abstract may leave out the detail that decides whether a claim holds.

So retrieval needs a second step: **a check, claim by claim, of whether the retrieved evidence actually supports what the answer says**. Claims that pass get their sources attached. Claims that fail are flagged instead of being paired with the nearest citation.

## What trade-offs should builders expect?

- Time. A search per claim adds time to every answer, and an answer with many claims needs many searches.
- Query building. Turning a sentence into search terms is a skill. Synonyms, Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and the right level of detail all affect what comes back.
- Coverage. PubMed holds citations and abstracts, and full text is not always available. PubMed Central offers free full text for some articles.
- Ranking. The most relevant record is not always the first one returned, so the system needs to read beyond the top result.
- An outside dependency. Your product now relies on an external service, so plan for it being slow or unavailable.

These costs are real. What they buy is something a model's memory cannot provide: a source you can open, read and check.

## How does a claim-level retrieval check work in practice?

Luma is one example. It searches PubMed for each claim in an answer, judges whether the retrieved evidence supports the claim, attaches the source when it does and flags the claim when it does not. Unsupported claims are never dressed up with a citation, and each claim gets a confidence score. You can paste an answer into the [demo](/demo) to see the result.

Whatever you build, keep the retrieved record and the support judgment together, so a reviewer can open the same PMID and reach their own view.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a training cutoff in AI?

A training cutoff is the point after which no new text went into a model's training. A model answering from memory knows nothing published after that point, including new studies, updated guidelines and retractions.

### Can an AI model look up papers from its memory?

No. A model answering from memory generates text from learned patterns and keeps no record of which document a fact came from. To cite a real paper reliably, a system has to search a database such as PubMed and work from the records it retrieves.

### Does live retrieval stop AI hallucinations?

It reduces the risk of invented sources by giving the model real records to work from, but a model can still misstate what a retrieved paper says. A separate check of whether each source supports each claim is still needed.

### Why is PubMed a good source for live retrieval?

PubMed is a free search engine for biomedical literature maintained by the National Library of Medicine. Its records carry stable PubMed IDs and show retraction notices on retracted papers, so a system and a reader can check the same record.
