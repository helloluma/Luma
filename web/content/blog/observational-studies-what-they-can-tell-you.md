An observational study watches what happens to people without assigning them a treatment or exposure. It can show that two things tend to occur together and can follow people over long periods, but on its own it usually cannot prove that one thing causes the other.

## What is an observational study?

In an observational study, researchers record what people already do, what they are exposed to and what happens to their health. They do not decide who gets what.

That is the main difference from a randomized controlled trial, where researchers assign participants to groups by chance. Random assignment tends to balance the groups on everything else, measured or not, so a difference in outcomes can be traced to the treatment. Observational studies do not have that protection. The article on [randomized controlled trials](/blog/what-is-a-randomized-controlled-trial) explains how assignment by chance works.

## What are the main types of observational study?

Three designs come up most often:

- Cohort studies follow a group of people over time and compare outcomes between those with and without an exposure. A prospective cohort enrolls people now and follows them forward. A retrospective cohort uses records collected in the past. Because the exposure is measured before the outcome, a cohort study can establish the order of events.
- Case-control studies start with the outcome. Researchers identify people who have a condition (the cases) and similar people who do not (the controls), then look back at what each group was exposed to. This design suits rare conditions, since there is no need to wait for cases to appear. It depends on memory or old records, which can be incomplete, and people with a condition may recall their past differently from people without it.
- Cross-sectional studies measure exposure and outcome at the same moment, like a snapshot. They are good for describing how common something is in a population. They cannot show which came first.

Case reports and case series, which describe individual patients, are also observational. They are useful for spotting something new and weak at establishing what caused it.

## What can observational studies tell you?

Observational research answers questions that trials often cannot:

- Whether an exposure and an outcome tend to occur together, and how strongly
- Long-term effects that would take too long to study in a trial
- Rare side effects that a trial may be too small or too short to detect
- Questions that cannot ethically be tested by assigning people. Researchers cannot assign people to smoke, so questions like that have to be studied by observing people who already do.
- How treatments behave in everyday practice, across a wider range of people than a trial usually enrolls

## Why doesn't association mean causation?

Two things can travel together without one causing the other. Before reading an association as a cause, researchers try to rule out three explanations:

1. Confounding. A third factor drives both the exposure and the outcome. A classic teaching example: people who carry lighters are more likely to develop lung cancer. The lighters are harmless. Smoking explains both the lighter and the cancer.
2. Reverse causation. The outcome causes the exposure, instead of the other way around. In a hypothetical example, people who are starting to feel unwell may cut back on exercise. A study could then find less exercise among people who later receive a diagnosis and wrongly conclude that the lower activity caused the illness.
3. Chance and bias. Random variation can produce an association that means nothing. Bias can create one too: people who volunteer for health studies may differ from those who do not, and the way exposures are measured can tilt the results.

Researchers adjust for confounders they have measured, using statistical methods. **They cannot adjust for factors nobody measured**, and unmeasured confounding is the main reason observational results can mislead.

## When can observational evidence point to a cause?

Sometimes observation is all the evidence there is, and confidence in a causal link builds when several signs line up:

- The association is strong rather than slight.
- It appears consistently across studies in different populations, by different teams, using different designs.
- The exposure clearly comes before the outcome.
- More exposure goes with more effect.
- There is a plausible biological explanation.
- The link holds after adjusting for the main known confounders.

No single sign settles the question. Where observational work sits relative to trials and reviews is laid out in [the hierarchy of evidence](/blog/hierarchy-of-evidence), and [why one study is rarely the final word](/blog/why-one-study-is-rarely-the-final-word) explains why the weight of many studies matters more than any one result.

## How should you read news about an observational study?

When a headline reports a new study, a few habits help:

1. Check the wording. "Linked to" and "associated with" describe an association. "Causes" is a stronger claim, and headlines sometimes make that jump when the study did not.
2. Find out the design. The abstract usually says whether the study was a cohort, case-control or cross-sectional study.
3. Ask how big the difference is in absolute terms. A large relative change in a rare outcome can still be small for any one person, as explained in [relative risk versus absolute risk](/blog/relative-risk-vs-absolute-risk).
4. Ask what else could explain the result, starting with confounding and reverse causation.
5. Look for trials or reviews on the same question before treating one study as the answer.

If a study makes you wonder whether to change a medicine, your diet or a habit, talk it through with your clinician first. Population findings do not translate directly to any one person's situation.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is an example of an observational study?

A cohort study that follows a group of people over time and compares health outcomes between those who do and do not have a certain exposure is an observational study. Researchers record what happens but do not assign the exposure.

### What is the difference between a cohort study and a case-control study?

A cohort study starts with an exposure and follows people to see who develops an outcome. A case-control study starts with people who already have an outcome, compares them with similar people who do not, and looks back at their past exposures.

### Can observational studies prove causation?

On their own, rarely. Confounding, reverse causation, bias or chance can explain an association, so causal conclusions usually need consistent evidence from several studies and designs, including trials where trials are possible.

### What is confounding in research?

Confounding happens when a third factor is linked to both an exposure and an outcome, creating an association between them that does not reflect cause and effect. Smoking, for example, confounds any link between carrying a lighter and lung cancer.
