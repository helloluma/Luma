Fabricated references are still uncommon in biomedical papers, but they are showing up more often. An audit by Topaz and colleagues, published in The Lancet in 2026, found that roughly one in 277 papers published in the first seven weeks of 2026 cited a paper that does not exist, up from one in 458 in 2025 and one in 2,828 in 2023.

Here is what the audit checked, what it found, and what it means for the people who read, cite and publish biomedical research.

## What did the audit check?

The audit looked at 97.1 million structured references across about 2.5 million biomedical papers. In this context, a fabricated reference is a citation to a paper that does not exist. There is no published work behind it to find.

Scale matters here. Checking every reference by hand is slow work. An audit of this size shows what turns up when references are checked in bulk.

## What did the audit find?

The main findings, as reported by Topaz and colleagues:

- 4,046 fabricated references across 2,810 papers.
- Roughly one in 277 papers published in the first seven weeks of 2026 cited a paper that does not exist.
- That compares with one in 458 papers in 2025 and one in 2,828 in 2023.
- 98.4 percent of affected papers had received no publisher action at the time of the audit.

The audit also reports that prior studies estimate 30 to 69 percent of references generated by large language models in biomedical contexts are fabricated.

## How should you read these numbers?

Three points help put the findings in context.

First, **the trend is the most important part.** The share of papers citing a nonexistent paper went from one in 2,828 in 2023 to one in 458 in 2025, and to roughly one in 277 in the first seven weeks of 2026.

Second, the counts are of references to papers that do not exist. A reference can also point to a real paper that does not support the claim it is attached to. That is a different error, and it is not what these counts describe. We cover it in [why a real citation can still be the wrong citation](/blog/real-citation-wrong-claim).

Third, a count cannot tell you how any single fake reference got into its paper. The audit reports the estimate from prior studies on references generated by large language models alongside its own counts, but each affected paper has its own history.

## Why does the lack of publisher action matter?

The finding that 98.4 percent of affected papers had received no publisher action at the time of the audit means those references were still in the published record, uncorrected, when the audit was done.

Corrections and retractions exist so the scientific record can fix itself. When they do not happen, the burden shifts to every later reader. Someone who follows a fabricated reference finds nothing. An author who copies references from an earlier paper can carry a fake one forward into new work, where it looks just as credible as before.

Our post on [what happens when fabricated citations enter the published literature](/blog/fabricated-citations-in-published-literature) looks at those downstream effects in more detail.

## What does this mean for readers and authors?

If you rely on biomedical papers, treat a reference list as a set of claims you can check.

- Look up any reference you plan to cite yourself, using its PubMed ID (PMID) or its digital object identifier (DOI).
- Confirm that the authors, title, journal and year match the record.
- Read the abstract to confirm it supports the sentence the reference is attached to.
- Do not copy a citation from another paper's reference list without checking the original.
- If you use AI tools to help draft, check every reference they produce before it goes into your work.

Our guide on [how to check if a PubMed citation is real](/blog/how-to-check-if-a-pubmed-citation-is-real) walks through these steps one at a time.

## What does this mean for editors and reviewers?

Editors and peer reviewers sit at the point where a fake reference can still be stopped before publication. Practical steps include:

- An automated check that every reference resolves to a real record.
- A requirement that authors confirm each reference was checked against the source.
- Spot checks of the references attached to a paper's key claims.
- A clear process for correcting a published paper when a fabricated reference is found after publication.

We go into these steps in [how journal editors and peer reviewers can screen for fabricated references](/blog/editors-reviewers-screen-fabricated-references).

## What do these numbers not tell you?

The numbers above are the audit's findings, quoted as reported. They do not, on their own, tell you which journals, fields or kinds of paper are most affected, and they do not tell you whether any particular paper's main findings are wrong. A paper can contain one fabricated reference and still report sound results. It still deserves a correction, because readers who check its sources will hit a dead end.

### How many biomedical papers cite fabricated references?

### Are fabricated references becoming more common?

According to the same audit, yes. The rate went from one in 2,828 papers in 2023 to one in 458 in 2025, and to roughly one in 277 in the first seven weeks of 2026.

### Do publishers correct papers with fabricated references?

According to the audit by Topaz and colleagues, 98.4 percent of affected papers had received no publisher action at the time of the audit.

### How can I tell if a reference is fabricated?

Look it up by its PMID, DOI or exact title on PubMed or the publisher's site, and confirm that the authors, journal and year match. If no matching record exists, treat the reference as unverified and do not cite it.
