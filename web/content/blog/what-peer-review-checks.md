Peer review checks whether a study asks a clear question, uses suitable methods, and draws conclusions its results can support. It usually does not check the raw data, rerun the analysis, or verify every reference, so a peer-reviewed paper can still contain errors.

Peer review is a useful filter, and it catches some kinds of problems far better than others. Knowing which ones helps you decide how much extra checking a paper needs before you rely on it.

## What is peer review?

Before a journal publishes a research paper, the editor usually sends it to a few experts in the same field. These reviewers read the manuscript and advise the editor to accept it, reject it, or ask the authors for changes. The editor makes the final decision, often after one or more rounds of revision.

Journals run the process in different ways. In some, reviewers know the authors' names but the authors do not know who reviewed them. In others, neither side knows. Some journals publish the reviews alongside the paper. Reviewers are usually working researchers who review in addition to their own jobs.

## What do peer reviewers check?

Reviewers judge the paper as a piece of reasoning. They ask questions like these:

- **Is the question clear and worth asking?** Does the paper explain what is already known and what gap it fills?
- **Do the methods fit the question?** Is the study design suitable, is the sample described, and are the measurements appropriate?
- **Do the conclusions follow from the results?** Reviewers look for claims that go beyond the data, such as treating an association as proof of cause and effect.
- **Is the analysis reasonable?** Reviewers check whether the statistical approach suits the data. Some journals also send papers to a dedicated statistical reviewer.
- **Are the limitations stated?** A good paper says what it could not show.
- **Is the paper clear and complete?** Reviewers catch confusing writing, missing details, and figures that do not match the text.
- **Is ethical review reported?** For studies in people, the paper should describe ethics approval and consent.

## What does peer review usually miss?

Reviewers work from the manuscript, and that limits what they can see.

- **Raw data.** Reviewers usually see summary results, not the underlying data, so they generally cannot confirm that every number was calculated correctly. Some journals ask authors to share data, but reviewers are rarely in a position to rerun a full analysis.
- **Every reference.** Reviewers seldom look up each citation to confirm that it exists and supports the sentence it is attached to. A wrong or invented reference can pass unnoticed.
- **Deliberate fraud.** Review assumes good faith. Fabricated or manipulated data designed to look normal is hard to detect from a manuscript alone.
- **Replication.** Peer review judges the paper. It does not repeat the experiment, so a finding can pass review and still fail to hold up when others test it. See [why one study is rarely the final word](/blog/why-one-study-is-rarely-the-final-word).
- **Problems found later.** Errors discovered after publication are handled through corrections and retractions. PubMed shows retraction notices on the records of retracted papers, and [how to check whether a paper has been retracted](/blog/how-to-check-if-a-paper-was-retracted) walks through the steps.

## How often do invented references reach published papers?

References are a clear example of something review is not set up to check. An audit published in The Lancet in 2026 checked 97.1 million structured references across about 2.5 million biomedical papers and found 4,046 fabricated references across 2,810 papers.

The rate has been climbing. Roughly one in 277 papers published in the first seven weeks of 2026 cited a paper that does not exist, up from one in 458 in 2025 and one in 2,828 in 2023. The audit also found that 98.4 percent of affected papers had received no publisher action at the time of the audit.

The same audit reports that prior studies estimate 30 to 69 percent of references generated by large language models in biomedical contexts are fabricated. Practical screening steps for editors and reviewers are in [how journal editors and peer reviewers can screen for fabricated references](/blog/editors-reviewers-screen-fabricated-references).

## Does peer review mean a study is correct?

No. It means experts read the paper and judged it fit to publish, often after asking for changes. A peer-reviewed study can still be small, early or wrong, and later research may change its conclusion.

Preprints sit at the other end of the process. They are papers posted publicly before any peer review. They help new findings circulate quickly, but their conclusions should be treated as provisional until review is complete. [Preprints: what they are and how to treat them](/blog/what-is-a-preprint) explains more.

## How should you read a peer-reviewed paper?

Treat peer review as a starting point and add your own checks where it matters.

1. Read the methods, not only the conclusion. Note who was studied, how many people took part, and how outcomes were measured.
2. Check that the conclusion matches the results and the stated limitations.
3. Look up any reference you plan to rely on, and confirm it supports the sentence that cites it.
4. Check the PubMed record for corrections or retraction notices.
5. Look for newer studies or a systematic review on the same question.

If you are writing about a study for others, these steps are part of the job. A paper's passage through peer review tells your reader that experts found it worth publishing. Your own checks tell them the specific claim you are repeating is supported.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does peer review check the raw data?
Usually not. Reviewers generally see the manuscript and its summary results, so they cannot confirm that every number was calculated correctly from the underlying data.

### Do peer reviewers check every reference?
Rarely. Reviewers focus on the question, methods and conclusions, so a wrong or invented reference can pass review unnoticed.

### Can a peer-reviewed paper be retracted?
Yes. Papers can be corrected or retracted after publication when serious problems are found, and PubMed shows retraction notices on the records of retracted papers.

### Is a preprint peer reviewed?
No. A preprint is posted before peer review, so its conclusions should be treated as provisional until it has been reviewed and published.
