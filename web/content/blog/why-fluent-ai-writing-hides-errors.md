Fluent writing makes AI medical errors harder to spot because the polish is the same on every sentence, right or wrong. Most readers treat smooth, confident prose as a sign that the writer knows the subject, and AI output sends that signal everywhere, so a false claim arrives looking exactly like a true one.

## Why does polished writing feel trustworthy?

People judge writing by its surface before they check its content: clean grammar, a steady tone, precise terms, tidy formatting. In human writing those cues are imperfect but useful. A writer who knows a subject well usually writes about it clearly. A writer who is out of their depth often shows it, with vague wording, hedges in odd places or a sudden change in tone.

Readers learn to use those cues as shortcuts. A shaky paragraph invites a second look. A confident, well-organized one usually gets waved through.

With text from a large language model, the shortcut stops working. The surface cues are present whether the content is right or not.

## Why does AI write wrong things in the same voice as right things?

A large language model produces text by predicting likely next words. Fluency is what that process does best. Accuracy depends on whether the patterns the model learned happen to match the facts, and nothing in the writing step reports when they do not. The article on [what an AI hallucination in medicine is](/blog/what-is-an-ai-hallucination-in-medicine) covers where those errors come from.

The result is uniform polish. A correct statement about a well-studied drug, a half-remembered claim and an invented reference all come out with the same grammar, tone and format. Human writers leave traces of doubt in their prose. A model writing from patterns leaves none unless it has been specifically designed to.

## What kinds of errors hide behind fluent text?

Several kinds of error survive a quick read because they look like everything around them:

- Invented references. A fabricated citation has authors, a year, a journal and page numbers in the right order. Formatting is the easy part.
- Real sources attached to the wrong claims. A real paper is cited for a finding it does not report, the problem described in [why a real citation can still be the wrong citation](/blog/real-citation-wrong-claim).
- Hedges turned into certainty. "May be associated with" in the source becomes "causes" in the answer.
- Precise-looking details. A specific number, a named study or an exact date reads as reliable, even when it was invented.
- Small swaps inside true sentences. A sentence that correctly says angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors can cause a dry cough reads exactly like one that pairs a drug with the wrong side effect.

## What did a 2023 study find about chatbot citations?

A 2023 study by Walters and Wilder in Scientific Reports examined 636 citations across 84 literature reviews generated by an earlier version and a newer version of a widely used chatbot, on 42 topics. In that study, 55 percent of the citations from the earlier version and 18 percent from the newer version were fabricated. Among the real citations, 43 percent of the earlier version's and 24 percent of the newer version's contained substantive errors.

The study covered topics from many fields and was not specific to medicine, so its numbers are not a rate for medical answers. Its relevance here is simpler. A citation's format carries no information about whether it is real, and only checking tells you which kind you are looking at. The full findings are in [what the 2023 study of chatbot citations found](/blog/what-the-2023-chatbot-citation-study-found).

## How can you read past the polish?

Reading AI output well means separating how it sounds from what it says:

1. List the claims. Pull the factual statements out of the prose one at a time, the idea behind [claim-level verification](/blog/what-is-claim-level-verification). A claim on its own line is much easier to question than one buried in a smooth paragraph.
2. Treat formatting as zero evidence. A neat reference list tells you nothing about whether the references exist.
3. Look hardest at the specific details: numbers, names, dates and descriptions of studies. They are the easiest details to invent and the hardest to notice.
4. Check each source yourself. Look up the identifier, compare the details and read the abstract against the sentence it is meant to support.
5. Notice when an answer never hedges. Medical evidence is often uncertain, and text that admits no uncertainty at all is a reason to look closer.

If you are reading AI output about your own health, take your questions to your clinician rather than acting on the answer.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why do AI medical answers sound so confident?

A large language model produces fluent text by predicting likely words, and the tone of its writing does not depend on whether the content is correct. That makes wrong and right statements sound equally sure.

### How can you tell if an AI health answer contains errors?

You usually cannot tell from the writing alone. Break the answer into individual claims and check each one against a reliable source, paying most attention to specific numbers, names and references.

### Are AI-generated references usually real?

Not reliably. In a 2023 study of literature reviews written by an earlier and a newer version of a widely used chatbot, 55 percent of the earlier version's citations and 18 percent of the newer version's were fabricated, across topics from many fields rather than medicine alone.

### Does better writing quality mean an AI tool is more accurate?

No. Fluency and accuracy come from different things, so a tool can write well while getting facts or sources wrong, and accuracy has to be checked against the sources themselves.
