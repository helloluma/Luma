Accuracy benchmarks are not enough for medical AI because they score whether the final answer is right, and they do not check whether the claims and sources behind it hold up. A model can pick the correct option on an exam-style question and still, in open-ended use, cite papers that do not exist or do not say what it claims.

For builders, a benchmark score answers one question and leaves the questions users care about most unmeasured. Sourcing and faithfulness need their own measurements.

## What do accuracy benchmarks measure?

A typical accuracy benchmark is a set of questions with an answer key, often in multiple-choice or short-answer form. The system answers each question, the answers are compared with the key, and the result is a single number: the share it got right.

That number is useful. It tells you something about what a system knows and lets you compare systems on the same task. It does not tell you how the system explains itself, what it cites, or what it does when the honest answer is uncertain.

## Why doesn't a high score mean a model cites well?

Picking an answer and supporting one are different tasks. On a multiple-choice question, the options are given, and only the final pick is graded. In real use, people ask open questions and get paragraphs back, with claims and references the benchmark never looks at.

Citation quality has to be measured directly, and when it is, the results can be poor. Walters and Wilder, writing in Scientific Reports in 2023, examined 636 citations across 84 literature reviews generated by an earlier version and a newer version of a widely used chatbot, on 42 topics from many fields, not only medicine. Fabricated citations made up 55 percent of the earlier version's citations and 18 percent of the newer version's. Among the real citations, 43 percent of the earlier version's and 24 percent of the newer version's contained substantive errors.

A reference that exists but carries a substantive error is invisible to an accuracy score. So is a correct-sounding sentence backed by a paper that studied a different population.

## What is faithfulness, and how do you measure it?

Faithfulness asks whether each claim in an answer is supported by the source it cites, or by the evidence the system retrieved. An answer can be accurate and unfaithful at once, for example when the conclusion is right but the cited paper says something else.

Measure it claim by claim:

1. Split the answer into individual factual claims.
2. For each claim, find the source the system attached to it.
3. Confirm the source exists and its details match.
4. Judge whether the source supports the claim for the same population, intervention and outcome.
5. Record each claim as supported, partly supported, not supported, or backed by a source that does not exist.

The first and fourth steps are the hard ones. [Claim-level verification](/blog/what-is-claim-level-verification) explains the first, and [how to tell whether a study actually supports a claim](/blog/how-to-tell-if-a-study-supports-a-claim) covers the fourth.

## What should a sourcing evaluation report?

Alongside accuracy, report measures like these, each with the method used to judge it:

- The share of references that resolve to a real record
- The share of claims whose cited source supports them
- How many unsupported claims the system flagged, compared with how many it presented as supported
- How often the system declines to answer when the evidence is thin, and whether those declines were warranted
- How consistent claims and sources are across repeated runs of the same question
- Whether stated confidence matches how often the system is actually right, which is the idea behind [calibration](/blog/what-is-calibration-in-medical-ai)

A system can do poorly on any of these while scoring well on accuracy.

## Why does abstention belong in the scorecard?

If a benchmark scores "I don't know" the same as a wrong answer, a system never gains anything by declining. Under that rule, guessing is always at least as good as abstaining, so the score cannot tell you whether the system knows its limits.

In medicine, a confident guess on a question with thin evidence does more harm than an honest flag. **Give credit for a warranted abstention and a penalty for an unsupported claim stated as fact,** and the scorecard starts to reward the behavior you want.

## How should builders report evaluation results?

- Report accuracy next to sourcing and faithfulness, never alone.
- Describe how claims were judged, by whom, and with what instructions.
- Have clinical reviewers grade outputs without knowing which system produced them. [Blinded clinician review](/blog/why-blinded-clinician-review-matters) keeps expectations from coloring the grades.
- Show worked examples of failures along with the summary numbers.
- Retest when the underlying model changes. The study above found large differences between two versions of the same chatbot.

Luma is built around this kind of check. It splits an answer into claims, searches PubMed for each one, judges whether the evidence supports it, gives each claim a confidence score and flags what it cannot verify.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a medical AI benchmark?

A medical AI benchmark is a fixed set of questions, often exam-style, with an answer key used to score and compare systems. The usual result is a single accuracy number.

### Can a model with a high benchmark score still make up references?

Yes. Benchmarks that grade only the final answer never check the references a model gives in open-ended use, so fabricated or misattributed citations go unmeasured.

### What is faithfulness in medical AI?

Faithfulness means each claim in an answer is supported by the source it cites or by the evidence the system retrieved. It is measured claim by claim, separately from whether the overall answer is correct.

### What should builders measure besides accuracy?

Measure whether references resolve, whether cited sources support each claim, how the system handles thin evidence, how consistent it is across runs, and whether its confidence matches how often it is right.
