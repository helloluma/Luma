A study supports a claim only when it looked at the same kind of people, the same intervention or exposure, and the same outcome the claim describes, and found an effect in the same direction with at least the certainty the claim expresses. If any of those pieces differ, the citation may be real while the claim goes further than the study does.

The checklist below works on an abstract in a few minutes, and it gets more reliable when you can read the full paper.

## Why isn't finding the study enough?

Confirming that a paper exists is the first step, covered in [how to check if a PubMed citation is real](/blog/how-to-check-if-a-pubmed-citation-is-real). Existence tells you nothing about whether the paper backs the sentence it is attached to. A real citation can still be paired with a claim it does not support, and we look at how that happens in [why a real citation can still be the wrong citation](/blog/real-citation-wrong-claim).

Start by writing the claim down as one plain sentence. Then go through the checks in order.

## What should you check, step by step?

### 1. Same population?

Who was studied? Adults or children, a particular age range, people with a specific condition, hospital patients or the general public. A finding in one group does not automatically carry over to another. If the claim says "people with diabetes" and the study enrolled people without diabetes, the study does not support the claim as written.

Also check whether the study involved humans at all. Lab and animal studies can show a possible mechanism, but they do not show that the same effect happens in people.

### 2. Same intervention or exposure?

Check exactly what was tested: which drug, in which form, for how long, or which exposure. A claim about a whole class of drugs is not supported by a study of one member of that class. A claim about long-term use is not supported by a short study.

### 3. Same outcome?

Studies measure specific outcomes. A trial might report a change in a blood test value, while the claim talks about heart attacks. A change in a lab marker does not automatically mean a change in the outcomes people care about, such as symptoms, hospital stays or survival.

### 4. Same direction and size of effect?

Did the study find the effect the claim describes, the opposite effect, or no clear difference? Check the size too. A claim that something "greatly reduces" risk needs a study that found a meaningful reduction, and statistical significance alone does not show that. Our post on [what "statistically significant" does and does not mean](/blog/what-statistically-significant-means) explains the difference.

### 5. Right study design for the claim?

Causal language such as "prevents," "causes" or "cures" needs a design that can support cause and effect, usually a randomized controlled trial or a systematic review of trials. An observational study can show that two things are associated, but other factors may explain the link. See [the hierarchy of evidence](/blog/hierarchy-of-evidence) for how designs compare.

### 6. Hedged or firm conclusion?

Read the conclusion of the abstract carefully. Authors who write "may be associated with," "suggests" or "further research is needed" are telling you the finding is tentative. **If the claim states the same idea as settled fact, it has gone further than its source.**

### 7. Main finding or side note?

Check whether the claim relies on the study's primary outcome or on a secondary analysis, a small subgroup, or a sentence from the background section. Background sentences describe other people's work. They are not the study's own finding, and citing them as if they were is an easy way for a claim to drift from its source.

## What does a mismatch look like in practice?

Here is a hypothetical example. An answer says, "Drug A reduces hospital admissions in older adults," and cites a trial. Reading the abstract, you find the trial enrolled middle-aged adults, measured a blood marker rather than admissions, and concluded that Drug A "may warrant further study."

The paper is real. The claim still fails three checks: population, outcome and certainty.

You have three options. Rewrite the claim to match what the study found, find a source that supports the original wording, or remove the claim.

## How do you record the result?

For each claim, write down one of three outcomes:

- Supported: the study matches on every check.
- Partly supported: it matches on some checks. Note which ones fail and narrow the claim to fit.
- Not supported: the study does not back the claim. Find another source or cut the claim.

A short record like this makes your work easy to review, and it lets a colleague repeat the check without starting over. It also shows you patterns: if most of your problems are population mismatches, you know where to look first next time.

## When is the abstract not enough?

An abstract is enough for a first pass on population, outcome, direction and certainty. Go to the full paper when the claim depends on a specific number, a subgroup, a side effect, or a detail of how the intervention was given. Abstracts are short, and they often leave out limitations, subgroup details and many secondary results.

If the full text is not free, the PubMed record will often link to the publisher's page, and some articles have a free version in PubMed Central.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does it mean for a study to support a claim?

It means the study examined the same population, intervention and outcome the claim describes, found an effect in the same direction, and reached a conclusion at least as firm as the claim. A real citation that differs on any of these does not fully support the claim.

### Can an observational study support a causal claim?

Usually not on its own. An observational study can find an association, but other factors can explain the link, so causal claims generally need randomized trials or reviews of trials.

### Is the abstract enough to judge whether a study supports a claim?

The abstract is enough for a first check of population, outcome, direction and certainty. For claims about specific numbers, subgroups or side effects, read the full paper.

### What should I do if a study only partly supports a claim?

Narrow the claim so it matches what the study found, or find a source that supports the original wording. Do not keep the original claim with a citation that only partly backs it.
