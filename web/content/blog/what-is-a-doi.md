A digital object identifier (DOI) is a permanent, unique identifier assigned to a published work, such as a journal article, so the work can be found even if its web address changes. To use one, type doi.org/ followed by the DOI into your browser's address bar, and the resolver will send you to the work's current page, usually on the publisher's site.

A DOI is also one of the fastest ways to check whether a citation points to a real paper.

## What does a DOI look like?

Every DOI starts with "10." and has two parts separated by a slash:

- The prefix, which begins with 10. and identifies the organization that registered the DOI, often the publisher.
- The suffix, after the slash, which the registrant chooses to identify the specific item.

For example, the 2023 study of chatbot citations by Walters and Wilder in Scientific Reports has the DOI 10.1038/s41598-023-41032-5. The prefix is 10.1038, and the suffix is s41598-023-41032-5. You can open it at [doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-41032-5](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-41032-5).

In a reference list, a DOI may appear with a "doi:" label in front, or as a full link that begins with doi.org. Both forms point to the same work.

## Who assigns DOIs?

DOIs are issued through registration agencies. Crossref is one such agency, used by many scholarly publishers to register DOIs for their articles. The publisher assigns the DOI, records its details with the agency (title, authors, journal and dates), and keeps the link pointing to the article's current location.

Persistence comes from that arrangement. If a journal moves to a new website, the publisher updates the record, and the same DOI keeps working.

DOIs are used for more than journal articles. Books, chapters, datasets, conference papers and reports can have them too.

## How do you use a DOI to find a paper?

1. Copy the DOI exactly, starting from "10." and leaving off any "doi:" label in front.
2. Type doi.org/ followed by the DOI into your browser's address bar. The resolver at [doi.org](https://www.doi.org/) sends you to the work's current page.
3. Check that the page you land on matches the citation: title, authors, journal and year.
4. To find the PubMed record as well, paste the DOI into the PubMed search box. For biomedical articles, this often brings up the matching record.

If the publisher's page is behind a paywall, the PubMed record may link to a free full-text version in PubMed Central, when one exists.

## What does it mean if a DOI does not resolve?

If doi.org reports that the DOI cannot be found, treat that as a red flag. Possible reasons include:

- A typo, such as a missing character or a swapped digit.
- A DOI that was registered very recently and is not active yet.
- A DOI that was never registered, because the reference was made up.

Check the characters carefully against the source you copied from, then search for the title. **A DOI that does not resolve, combined with a title you cannot find anywhere, is a strong sign the citation is fabricated.** AI tools can produce DOIs that follow the correct format, sometimes with a real publisher's prefix, that point to nothing.

A DOI that does resolve still needs one more check. It can lead to a real paper that is not the one cited, if a digit has been altered or the DOI was taken from a different reference. Always compare the page you land on with the citation's title and authors. Our guide on [how to spot a citation that has been subtly altered](/blog/how-to-spot-an-altered-citation) covers this pattern.

## What is the difference between a DOI and a PubMed ID (PMID)?

Both are identifiers, but they come from different systems.

- A DOI is registered by the publisher through a registration agency. DOIs are used across academic fields, not only medicine, and they lead to the publisher's page.
- A PMID is a number that the National Library of Medicine assigns to each record in PubMed. It exists only for works that PubMed lists, and it leads to the PubMed record with the citation and abstract.

Many biomedical articles have both, and the PubMed record often shows the DOI. Older articles may have a PMID but no DOI, and articles outside the life sciences may have a DOI but no PMID.

When a citation gives both, check that they lead to the same paper. A mismatch between the DOI and the PMID is a sign that something in the reference was changed or mixed up. Our guide to [what a PMID is](/blog/what-is-a-pmid) explains the PubMed side in more detail.

## How does a DOI help when checking AI-generated references?

A DOI gives you a quick first test: does it resolve, and does it lead to the cited paper? Run that test on every reference in an AI-drafted list before you rely on any of it.

Resolving is only the start. A real paper can still be cited for a claim it does not make, so read the abstract and confirm it supports the sentence the reference is attached to. ## Frequently asked questions

### What does DOI stand for?

DOI stands for digital object identifier. It is a permanent identifier for a published work, such as a journal article, dataset or book chapter.

### How do I find a paper from its DOI?

Type doi.org/ followed by the DOI into your browser's address bar. The resolver sends you to the work's current page, usually on the publisher's website.

### What does it mean if a DOI does not work?

It may contain a typo or be newly registered, but it can also mean the reference was made up. Check the characters carefully, then search for the title, and if neither turns up the paper, treat the citation as unverified.

### Is a DOI the same as a PMID?

No. A DOI is registered by the publisher and is used across academic fields, while a PMID is assigned by the National Library of Medicine to records in PubMed.
