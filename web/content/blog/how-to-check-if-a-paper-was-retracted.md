To check whether a paper has been retracted, find its record on PubMed and look for a retraction notice, then open the article on the journal's own site and look for a retraction label or a linked notice. Do this for every paper you cite, including papers an AI tool suggested, because a retracted paper keeps circulating in text written before the notice appeared.

## What does it mean when a paper is retracted?

A retraction is a formal notice that a published paper should no longer be relied on. Journals retract papers for serious problems, such as errors that undermine the results, data that cannot be verified, plagiarism or misconduct. Authors sometimes ask for a retraction themselves after finding a mistake in their own work.

A retracted paper usually stays online, clearly marked, with a separate notice that explains why it was withdrawn. Keeping it visible lets readers see what was published and why it should not be used.

Two other notices are worth knowing:

- A correction, sometimes called an erratum, fixes a specific error. The paper still stands.
- An expression of concern tells readers that the editors have doubts about a paper while the question is still open.

Neither one withdraws the paper, but both are worth reading before you rely on it.

## How do you check for a retraction on PubMed?

PubMed is a free search engine for biomedical literature, maintained by the National Library of Medicine at the National Institutes of Health. When a paper indexed in PubMed is retracted, its record carries a retraction notice and links to the published notice. The article on [what PubMed is and who maintains it](/blog/what-is-pubmed) explains what a record contains.

1. Find the record on [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Search by the PubMed ID (PMID) if you have one. Otherwise search the exact title.
2. Confirm it is the right paper. The authors, journal and year should match the reference you started with.
3. Read the record for a retraction notice and for links to corrections or other notices about the paper.
4. Open the retraction notice and read the reason given.

Treat PubMed as a strong first check rather than the last word. Not every journal is indexed in PubMed, and a notice may take time to reach the record.

## How do you check on the journal's website?

Go to the article's page on the publisher's site. If you have the digital object identifier (DOI), resolving it through the [DOI system](https://www.doi.org/) takes you there. On that page, look for:

- A label or banner marking the article as retracted
- A watermark or stamp on the PDF
- A link to a retraction notice, a correction or an expression of concern

Retraction notices are often published as separate items whose titles repeat the original title, so searching the title can turn up both the paper and the notice. If the journal page and the PubMed record disagree, **treat the paper as retracted until you can confirm otherwise**, and note the discrepancy.

## Why do AI tools cite retracted papers?

A few reasons stack up:

- A large language model learns from text gathered up to a point in time. If the paper was retracted after that, or if the text the model learned from rarely mentioned the retraction, the model has no way to know.
- Text that cites a paper does not disappear when the paper is retracted. Earlier reviews, news stories and other papers that cited it remain in circulation.
- A model can reproduce a retracted paper's authors and title correctly, so the reference passes a quick check for existence.
- Even a system that searches the literature at answer time may read an abstract without checking the status attached to the record.

That is why a model's memory is a poor guide to the current state of the literature, a point covered in [why live literature retrieval beats a model's memory](/blog/live-literature-retrieval-vs-model-memory). ## What should you do if a paper you cited was retracted?

The right move depends on where your work is:

- Not yet published: remove the citation. Then check whether the claim it supported still stands on other evidence. If nothing else supports it, rewrite the claim or cut it.
- Already published: contact the editor or whoever controls the content, explain which reference is affected, and ask how they handle corrections.
- Writing about the retraction itself: cite both the paper and the retraction notice, and say plainly that the paper was retracted.

Do not cite a retracted paper as support for its own findings. Record the date you checked each reference. Status can change after you look, so on long projects, check again shortly before publication. ## Frequently asked questions

### Does PubMed show if an article has been retracted?

Yes. When a paper indexed in PubMed is retracted, its record carries a retraction notice and links to the published notice. Check the journal's own page as well, since not every journal is indexed in PubMed.

### Are retracted papers deleted?

Usually not. A retracted paper typically stays online, clearly marked as retracted, alongside a separate notice that explains why it was withdrawn.

### What is the difference between a retraction and a correction?

A correction fixes a specific error while the paper still stands. A retraction tells readers the paper should no longer be relied on.

### Why would an AI tool cite a retracted paper?

A language model learns from text gathered up to a point in time, including papers and the sources that cited them before any retraction. Unless a tool checks each record's current status, it can cite retracted work as if nothing had happened.
