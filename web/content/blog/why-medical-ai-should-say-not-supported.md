Medical AI should be allowed to say "not supported" because an honest flag on a claim it cannot back up is safer and more useful than a confident sentence that may be wrong. When a product hides its gaps, the gaps remain, and the risk shifts to a reader who has no way to tell the guess from the fact.

Below is the design case for abstention: letting the system decline to back a claim, and showing that decision clearly.

## Why do AI systems guess instead of abstaining?

A language model always produces text. Fluent generation has no built-in pause for "I am not sure," so the default behavior is to write something plausible. If a product puts that text in front of users unfiltered, every gap in the model's knowledge comes out as a smooth, confident sentence.

Product pressure can push in the same direction. A blank or hedged answer can feel like a failure in a demo, and a team that measures only whether users got an answer will tune the system toward always answering.

## What does a confident guess cost in health?

In many kinds of software, a wrong answer is an annoyance. In health information, people act on what they read. A clinician drafting a patient handout, a writer preparing a review, and a patient reading about a condition all take a confident sentence at face value, because nothing on the page tells them not to.

Invented references are the clearest case. An audit by Topaz and colleagues, published in The Lancet in 2026, reports that prior studies estimate 30 to 69 percent of references generated by large language models in biomedical contexts are fabricated. A system that attaches a citation to every claim, whether or not it found support, builds that problem into its design.

## What should "not supported" actually mean?

A useful flag says something specific. "Not supported" should mean the system looked for evidence for this claim and did not find a source that backs it.

Saying a claim is unsupported is different from saying it is false. Products can make the difference clear by separating cases such as:

- Supported: a source was found, and it backs the claim.
- Not supported: the search found nothing that backs the claim.
- Contradicted: a source was found that says the opposite.
- Out of scope: the claim is not something the literature can settle, such as a personal recommendation.

Each case tells the reader something different about what to do next. A contradicted claim should probably come out. An unsupported one needs a source or a softer wording.

## Why should products surface flags instead of hiding them?

One way to handle uncertainty is to quietly drop the claims the system cannot support. The answer looks cleaner, but the reader loses information. They never learn that a claim was considered and removed, and they may keep believing it from somewhere else.

**Surfacing the flag turns a hidden weakness into a visible task.** A writer sees exactly which sentence needs a source. A reviewer knows where to spend time. A patient knows which point to raise with a clinician.

Visible flags also keep a product honest with itself. When a team can see how often its system flags claims, it can tell whether a change made the system more careful or just more confident.

## How do you design a good abstention?

### Check at the claim level

Abstention works best one claim at a time. An answer can be mostly right with one unsupported sentence, so grading the whole answer as pass or fail throws away the information a reader needs. See [what claim-level verification is](/blog/what-is-claim-level-verification).

### Never decorate an unsupported claim

If no source supports a claim, do not attach the closest-looking paper. A real citation on the wrong claim is harder to catch than a missing one, as we explain in [why a real citation can still be the wrong citation](/blog/real-citation-wrong-claim).

### Make the flag visible and specific

Put the flag next to the claim it applies to, in plain words. Say what was checked and what was not found. A general disclaimer at the bottom of the page applies to everything, so it helps the reader with nothing in particular.

### Show confidence honestly

If you show a confidence score, it should mean something. A system that says it is fairly sure should be right about that often. Our post on [calibration in medical AI](/blog/what-is-calibration-in-medical-ai) covers why that matters.

### Treat abstention as a feature to measure

Track how often the system flags claims, and check a sample of those flags by hand. A system that almost never flags anything may be accurate, or it may simply be guessing with confidence. You only find out by looking.

### Write the flag for the person reading it

A patient and a medical writer need different words. For a writer, "no supporting source found in PubMed" is useful. For a patient, "we could not confirm this; ask your clinician" may be clearer. The underlying judgment is the same.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is abstention in medical AI?

Abstention is when an AI system declines to give or back a claim because it could not find enough support. In a well-designed product, the system shows the user that it abstained and on which claim.

### Does "not supported" mean a claim is false?

No. It means the system could not find a source that backs the claim, and a separate check is needed to show whether the claim is actually wrong.

### Will users trust a tool less if it flags claims?

A flag tells users exactly where to look, which makes the rest of the answer easier to rely on. A tool that never flags anything gives users no way to tell its guesses from its facts.

### How should a product show an unsupported claim?

Mark it next to the claim, in plain language, and say what was checked. Never attach a citation that does not support it.
