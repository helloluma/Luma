A PubMed ID (PMID) is the unique number PubMed assigns to each record in its database. To look one up, enter the number in the PubMed search box, or add it to the end of the PubMed web address, and the matching record opens.

PMIDs make citations easy to check. A title can be paraphrased and an author list can be shortened, but a PMID points to exactly one record.

## What does a PMID identify?

A PMID identifies a PubMed record. PubMed is the free search engine for biomedical literature maintained by the National Library of Medicine at the National Institutes of Health. Each record holds the citation details for one item, such as the title, authors, journal and publication date, and usually an abstract.

The PMID labels the record itself. A record may link to the full text at the publisher or in PubMed Central, but a PMID on its own does not mean free full text exists.

Because PubMed assigns PMIDs, the number comes from a public database instead of from the author of whatever text cites it. That independence is what makes it useful for checking.

## Where can you find a PMID?

- On the PubMed record itself, listed with the citation details.
- In the record's web address, which ends in the PMID.
- In reference lists, where some journals print the PMID after each reference.
- In citation managers, which can store it with each saved item.

For example, the 2026 audit of references in biomedical papers by Topaz and colleagues, published in The Lancet, has the PMID 42107362. Its record lives at [https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42107362/](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42107362/), and the number at the end of the address is the PMID.

## How do you look up a PMID?

1. Copy the number exactly, with no spaces or extra characters.
2. Enter it in the PubMed search box and run the search. The matching record should come up.
3. Or type the PubMed web address followed by the number, as in the example above.
4. Compare the record with the citation you are checking: title, authors, journal, year and volume.

If the search returns nothing, check for a copying error first. If the number was copied correctly and the record is missing, or the record describes a different paper, the citation has a problem.

## Why is a PMID a good anchor for checking a citation?

A citation is a claim that a specific paper exists. The PMID is the shortest route to testing that claim. Searching by title can return near matches and leave you guessing whether a small difference is a typo. **Searching by PMID gives one record**, and the record either matches the citation or it does not.

Mismatches stand out. A fabricated reference can carry a real PMID that belongs to an unrelated paper, so the check that matters is whether the record matches every detail of the citation, beyond whether the number exists. The full method is in [how to check if a PubMed citation is real](/blog/how-to-check-if-a-pubmed-citation-is-real).

Fabricated references are a real concern in published research. The Topaz audit checked 97.1 million structured references across about 2.5 million biomedical papers and found 4,046 fabricated references across 2,810 papers.

## What does a PMID not tell you?

A PMID confirms that a record exists. It does not tell you:

- Whether the paper supports the claim it is cited for. You still have to read it, as explained in [why a real citation can still be the wrong citation](/blog/real-citation-wrong-claim).
- Whether the paper has been retracted. Check the record for a retraction notice, which PubMed shows on retracted records.
- How strong the evidence is. A single case report and a large trial both have PMIDs.
- Whether free full text is available.

## How is a PMID different from a PubMed Central ID and a digital object identifier?

These three identifiers can appear side by side in a reference, and each labels something different.

- A PMID labels a record in PubMed, which holds citations and abstracts.
- A PubMed Central ID labels a full-text article in PubMed Central, the free full-text archive run by the National Library of Medicine. It starts with the letters PMC. Not every paper with a PMID has one.
- A digital object identifier (DOI) is a persistent identifier for the article itself, registered through agencies such as Crossref. You can resolve one at [doi.org](https://www.doi.org/) to reach the publisher's page. DOIs cover journals well beyond biomedicine.

Read more in [what a DOI is, and how to use it to find a paper](/blog/what-is-a-doi) and [what PubMed Central is, and how it differs from PubMed](/blog/pubmed-central-vs-pubmed).

## What if a paper has no PMID?

Not every paper is in PubMed. Journals outside its scope, books, conference material and research from other fields may have no record. A missing PMID does not prove a citation is fake. Search by exact title, try the DOI, or check the journal's own site before drawing a conclusion.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does PMID stand for?

PMID stands for PubMed ID. It is the unique number assigned to each record in PubMed, the free search engine for biomedical literature maintained by the National Library of Medicine.

### Is a PMID the same as a DOI?

No. A PMID labels a record in PubMed, while a DOI is a persistent identifier for the article itself that resolves to the publisher's page. A paper can have both, only one, or neither.

### Does every medical paper have a PMID?

No. Only items with a PubMed record have PMIDs, so a missing PMID is a reason to search by title or DOI before deciding a paper is fake.

### Can a citation include a fake PMID?

Yes. A citation can carry an invented number or a real PMID that belongs to a different paper. Look the number up and confirm that the record matches the title, authors and journal in the citation.
