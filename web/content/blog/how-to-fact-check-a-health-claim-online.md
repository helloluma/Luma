To fact-check a health claim you read online, trace it back to its original source, check who is making it and why, and look up the study it rests on. Then compare it with what guidelines and the wider research say, and ask your clinician before acting on anything that affects your own health.

You do not need medical training for most of these steps. You need a few minutes, a web browser, and PubMed, a free search engine for biomedical literature maintained by the National Library of Medicine at the National Institutes of Health.

## What exactly is the claim?

Start by writing the claim down in one sentence. Headlines and posts often blur several claims together. A hypothetical headline such as "this spice fights inflammation and protects your memory" holds two claims, and each needs its own check.

Notice the strength of the wording. "Linked to," "may help" and "cures" make very different promises, and careful sources choose their words on purpose. Watch for a claim that sounds stronger than the evidence behind it.

## Who is making the claim, and why?

Look at who published the page and who wrote it.

- Is the author named, and do they have relevant training?
- Does the site sell a product connected to the claim?
- Is the page an advertisement, a sponsored post, or a press release written to promote a study?
- Does the page link to its sources, or does it only say "studies show"?
- Is there a date, so you can tell how current the information is?

A commercial interest does not make a claim false. It is a reason to look closer.

## Where did the claim come from?

Follow the links back. A social media post may cite a news article, which cites a press release, which describes a study. Each step can drop a caveat or turn "may" into "does." Go as far back as you can, ideally to the study itself.

If you cannot find any original source, treat the claim as unverified. A claim with no traceable source cannot be checked, however confident it sounds.

## How do you find and read the study?

Search PubMed for the study's title if you have it, or for a few key words from the claim together with an author's name or the journal. The record shows the title, authors, journal, date and usually an abstract, a short summary of the study written by its authors. [What PubMed is, and who maintains it](/blog/what-is-pubmed) explains what you will find there.

Once you have the record, check these things:

1. **Did the study involve people?** Some health headlines come from studies in cells or animals. Those results can suggest how something might work, but they do not show an effect in people. See [why animal and lab studies do not prove effects in people](/blog/why-animal-and-lab-studies-do-not-prove-effects-in-people).
2. **What kind of study was it?** A randomized controlled trial is designed to test cause and effect. An observational study can show an association, which may have other explanations. [What observational studies can and cannot tell you](/blog/observational-studies-what-they-can-tell-you) covers the difference.
3. **Who took part, and how many?** A small study, or one in a group of people very different from you, may not apply to you.
4. **What did the authors actually conclude?** Read the conclusion in the abstract and compare its wording with the headline. Words like "may" and "associated with" matter.
5. **Is there a notice on the record?** PubMed shows retraction notices on the records of retracted papers. A retracted study should not be relied on.

## What do guidelines and other research say?

One study is rarely the final word. Check whether the claim matches the wider evidence.

- Search PubMed for a systematic review on the same question. A systematic review gathers and assesses the studies on a topic together.
- Check whether a clinical practice guideline addresses the question. Guidelines summarize the evidence and are updated as it changes.
- Look at the dates. An older finding may have been refined by later work, and a brand-new one may not have been repeated yet.

If a single new study contradicts a large body of earlier evidence, it may be worth watching. It is also a reason to wait for more research and to talk with your clinician before changing anything.

## What if the claim came from an AI tool?

AI tools can state health claims fluently and attach sources that look real. Some of those sources do not exist, and some real ones do not say what the answer claims. Check an AI answer the same way you would check a website: find each source in PubMed and confirm it supports the specific sentence. There is more on this in [what patients should know before asking AI about their health](/blog/what-patients-should-know-before-asking-ai-about-health).

## When should you ask your clinician?

Before you act on any health claim that affects your own care. That includes starting or stopping a medication, supplement or treatment, changing your diet because of a health condition, or skipping a test or appointment. Bring the claim and its source with you.

A fact-check tells you how well a claim is supported in general. Your clinician can tell you whether it applies to you, given your health, your history and your other treatments.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the fastest way to check a health claim online?
Find the original study behind the claim and look it up in PubMed to see who was studied, what kind of study it was and what the authors concluded. Then ask your clinician whether it applies to you.

### How can I tell if a health website is trustworthy?
Look for named authors with relevant training, links to original research, clear dates, and no product sales tied to the claim. A site that says "studies show" without linking to them deserves more caution.

### Does a study in mice or cells prove a treatment works in people?
No. Lab and animal studies can suggest how something might work, but only studies in people can show whether it has an effect in people.

### Should I change my treatment based on something I read online?
Talk with your clinician before starting, stopping or changing any treatment. They can tell you whether a claim you read applies to your health.
