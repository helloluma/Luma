A 2023 study in Scientific Reports found that a widely used chatbot fabricated a large share of the citations in the literature reviews it wrote: 55 percent of the citations from an earlier version and 18 percent from a newer version. Among the citations that were real, many still contained substantive errors.

## Who ran the study, and what did it test?

The study was by Walters and Wilder and was [published in Scientific Reports in 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37679503/). It looked at literature reviews generated by an earlier version and a newer version of a widely used chatbot, and at the citations those reviews contained.

The scale was clearly defined: 636 citations across 84 generated literature reviews on 42 topics. The analysis separated citations to real works from fabricated ones, then looked for substantive errors in the citations that were real.

The topics came from many fields. The study was not specific to medicine, and that matters for how its numbers should be used.

## What did the study find?

The findings fall into two groups.

- Fabricated citations: 55 percent of the citations from the earlier version and 18 percent from the newer version were fabricated. These pointed to works that do not exist.
- Errors in real citations: among the citations that did point to real works, 43 percent of the earlier version's and 24 percent of the newer version's contained substantive errors.

The newer version did better on both counts. Even so, 18 percent of its citations in this study were fabricated, and 24 percent of its real citations still had substantive errors.

## Why are fabrication and citation errors separate problems?

A fabricated citation refers to nothing. If you look it up, you find no matching paper, or you find a different paper that shares a few details. Once you check, it is easy to rule out.

An error in a real citation is harder to catch. A quick search finds the paper, so the reference seems fine, and the mistake surfaces only when you compare the details closely. A reader who checks only whether a paper exists will miss this whole category.

The article on [what a fabricated citation is](/blog/what-is-a-fabricated-citation) covers the difference between invented references and real references attached to the wrong claim.

## Does this study apply to medical answers?

The numbers do not transfer directly. The study covered topics from many fields rather than medicine alone, so its percentages should not be quoted as a rate for medical answers. For biomedical numbers, a separate audit of published papers is discussed in [how common fabricated references are in biomedical papers](/blog/how-common-are-fabricated-references-biomedical-papers).

What carries over is the mechanism. A chatbot produced references to works that do not exist alongside references to works that do, and the only dependable way to tell them apart is to check each one. A medical question gives a chatbot no special protection against that.

## Why did the newer version still invent citations?

A language model writes by predicting likely text. A reference is text with a very predictable shape: authors, year, title, journal, volume and pages. A model can produce that shape whether or not a matching paper exists, because nothing in the writing step goes and looks the paper up.

Improvements can make invented references rarer, and this study saw a drop between versions. Writing from learned patterns still has no built-in check against the published record, so the problem shrinks without disappearing.

## What are the limits of the study?

Every study has a scope, and this one's is worth stating plainly:

- It tested one chatbot, at two points in its development. Other tools, and later versions, may behave differently.
- Its topics spanned many fields, so it does not measure performance on medical questions specifically.
- It measured citations in generated literature reviews. Other kinds of output, and tools that search a database before answering, raise different questions.
- Chatbots are updated often, so any rate from a single study describes a moment in time.

None of these limits weakens the main lesson. They mean the numbers belong to this study and should be quoted with that context.

## What should readers take from the study?

Treat every chatbot citation as unverified until you have found the paper yourself. For a single reference, look up the identifier, confirm the authors, title, journal and year, and read the abstract against the sentence it supports. The steps are laid out in [how to check if a PubMed citation is real](/blog/how-to-check-if-a-pubmed-citation-is-real).

Because real citations can carry errors too, finding the paper is only the first check. Confirm the details match and that the paper says what the sentence claims. For a whole list of references, the procedure in [how to verify an AI-generated reference list](/blog/verify-ai-generated-reference-list) takes you through each step and how to record the outcome.
