To spot a subtly altered citation, look up the paper by its identifier and by its title separately, then compare every field (authors, title, journal, year, volume, pages) against the record you find. Altered citations survive a quick glance because one detail is wrong while everything around it is real, so the check has to go field by field.

## What is a subtly altered citation?

A subtly altered citation refers to a real paper but gets one or more details wrong. It differs from a [fabricated citation](/blog/what-is-a-fabricated-citation), which points to a paper that does not exist at all.

Alterations happen for ordinary reasons: a typo, a reference copied from someone else's list, a citation manager that pulled the wrong record. They also happen when an AI tool writes references from memory. A language model can reproduce a real title while blending in the year, journal or authors of a different paper, and unless the system is built to look the paper up, nothing catches the mix.

The problem has been measured. A 2023 study examined literature reviews written by an earlier version and a newer version of a widely used chatbot. Among the citations that pointed to real works, 43 percent of the earlier version's and 24 percent of the newer version's contained substantive errors (Walters and Wilder, Scientific Reports). The reviews covered many fields, not medicine alone, but the pattern is the one medical writers and editors run into: a citation that looks right and is not.

## How do you catch a wrong year?

A wrong year is easy to miss because the rest of the citation checks out.

- Find the record in PubMed by title and compare the publication year.
- Check that the volume matches the year. A given volume of a journal belongs to a specific publication period, so a volume and year that do not line up with the record point to an error.
- Allow for online-first publication. A paper can appear online in one year and in a print issue the next. That is a legitimate difference, and the PubMed record often shows both dates.

## How do you catch a wrong journal?

A real title attached to the wrong journal often comes from a mix-up between similar journal names, or between a paper and a related commentary published somewhere else.

- Compare the full journal name, not only the abbreviation. Abbreviated journal titles can look alike.
- Check that the volume and page range make sense for that journal and year. A page range that belongs to a different issue is a signal.
- If the citation names one journal and the PubMed record names another, trust the record and work out where the error came from.

## How do you catch swapped or invented authors?

Author errors take several forms: the right names in the wrong order, a missing author, an extra author who was never on the paper, or a real researcher attached to a paper they did not write.

- Compare the first author exactly. Many reference styles and searches lean on the first author, so a wrong one can send readers to the wrong paper.
- Check the full author list against the record when the citation lists more than one name.
- Take extra care when an author's name is familiar in the field. A real, respected name makes an altered citation look more credible.

## How do you catch a real title with the wrong identifier?

This alteration is the hardest to catch, because both parts are real. The title belongs to one paper, and the PubMed ID (PMID) or digital object identifier (DOI) belongs to another paper, or to nothing.

1. Search the title in PubMed and note the PMID on the record you find.
2. Search the PMID from the citation on its own and note which paper it opens.
3. Resolve the DOI at [doi.org](https://www.doi.org/) and note where it lands.
4. All three should point to the same paper. If any one points elsewhere, the citation has been altered or miscopied.

A DOI that does not resolve at all is an obvious red flag. A DOI that resolves to a different paper is quieter and just as serious. For a refresher on the identifier itself, see [what a PMID is and how to look one up](/blog/what-is-a-pmid).

## Why do small citation errors matter?

A wrong year or a swapped author can look like a formatting problem. In practice it can send a reader to the wrong paper, make a real source impossible to find, or hide the fact that the cited paper does not say what the sentence claims.

The more extreme version of the problem is already in the published record. An audit published in The Lancet in 2026 checked 97.1 million structured references across about 2.5 million biomedical papers and found 4,046 fabricated references across 2,810 papers. Altered citations are harder to count than invented ones, because each one points to something that exists.

## What does a fast screening routine look like?

For a reference list you did not build yourself, run each citation through the same short routine:

1. Look up the identifier first, if there is one.
2. Search the title separately.
3. Compare authors, journal, year, volume and pages against the record.
4. Read the abstract and confirm it supports the sentence that cites it.
5. Record the outcome: confirmed, corrected or removed.

Step four matters even when every field matches. A perfectly formatted citation to a real paper can still be attached to a claim the paper never makes, which is the problem described in [why a real citation can still be the wrong citation](/blog/real-citation-wrong-claim).

Keeping a record of the outcome for each reference also helps the next person. An editor or co-author can see what was checked without repeating the work. For a complete procedure, see [how to verify an AI-generated reference list](/blog/verify-ai-generated-reference-list).

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between an altered citation and a fabricated one?
An altered citation points to a real paper but gets details such as the year, journal, authors or identifier wrong. A fabricated citation points to a paper that does not exist.

### Can a citation have a real PMID and still be wrong?
Yes. A real PMID can belong to a different paper than the title in the citation, so look up the PMID and the title separately and confirm they lead to the same record.

### Why do AI tools produce citations with small errors?
A language model writing references from memory produces text shaped like a citation, and it can blend details from different real papers. It does not look anything up unless the system around it is built to retrieve and check sources.

### What is the quickest way to check one citation?
Search the title in PubMed, compare every field against the record, and confirm the identifier opens the same paper. Then read the abstract to make sure it supports the sentence that cites it.
