A preprint is a complete research paper that its authors post publicly before it has been peer reviewed. Treat a preprint's conclusions as provisional: the work may be sound, but no independent reviewers have checked it yet, and the findings can change before a journal publishes them, if one ever does.

Preprints are a normal part of research and are often useful. The mistake is reading one as if it carried the same weight as a reviewed paper.

## What is a preprint?

A preprint is a manuscript the authors make public on a preprint server, a website that hosts papers before or alongside journal review. It usually has the full structure of a research paper: introduction, methods, results, discussion and references.

A few features are typical:

- **Free to read.** Anyone can open it.
- **A clear label.** Preprint servers generally mark papers as not yet peer reviewed.
- **Versions.** Authors can post revised versions, so the same preprint may exist in more than one form.
- **An identifier.** Many preprints receive a digital object identifier (DOI), which makes them easy to cite and find. Our explainer on [what a DOI is and how to use it to find a paper](/blog/what-is-a-doi) covers how that works.

## Why do researchers post preprints?

Journal review and publication take time. Posting a preprint lets researchers share findings as soon as the paper is written. Common reasons include:

- Getting results to other scientists quickly, which matters most when a field is moving fast, such as during an outbreak of a new disease.
- Inviting comments from other researchers before formal review.
- Creating a public, dated record of the work.

Speed is the benefit. It is also the risk, because the step that slows journals down is the step meant to catch problems.

## How is a preprint different from a peer-reviewed paper?

Peer review is the process in which a journal's editors send a manuscript to independent experts who examine its methods, analysis and conclusions. Reviewers can ask for more analysis, point out errors or recommend rejection. A paper that comes out the other side has at least been read critically by people outside the author team.

A preprint has skipped that step or has not reached it yet. That does not make it wrong. It means the checks a journal would apply have not been applied.

Peer review has limits of its own. Reviewers usually cannot rerun an experiment or check every reference, and flawed papers do get through. We cover what it catches and what it misses in [what peer review checks](/blog/what-peer-review-checks).

## How should you treat a preprint's findings?

Use these habits whenever a preprint comes up:

1. **Read it as provisional.** Treat the conclusions as a report of early work, open to revision.
2. **Check the version and date.** Make sure you are reading the latest version, and note when it was posted. See [why the date of a study matters](/blog/why-the-date-of-a-study-matters).
3. **Look for a published version.** Search PubMed for the title and the authors' names, since titles sometimes change during review. If a journal has published the work, read that version, because numbers and conclusions can change along the way. Many preprint servers also add a link once a journal version appears.
4. **Cite accurately.** If you cite a preprint, say it is a preprint. If a published version exists, cite that instead.
5. **Weigh it against other evidence.** One unreviewed paper should not outweigh a body of reviewed research. [Why one study is rarely the final word](/blog/why-one-study-is-rarely-the-final-word) explains why.
6. **Check whether it was withdrawn.** Authors can withdraw a preprint, and servers mark withdrawn papers. A withdrawn preprint can keep circulating in news stories and reference lists long after the authors stepped back from it.

## How do you tell whether something is a preprint?

The clues are usually in the citation or on the page:

- The source is a preprint server rather than a journal.
- The page carries a notice that the paper has not been peer reviewed.
- The citation names a server instead of a journal, volume and pages.
- News stories may report on a preprint without saying so, so follow the link to the original before you repeat a finding.

When you are unsure, look up the identifier. A DOI or link that resolves to a preprint server tells you what you are dealing with.

## Can AI tools confuse preprints with published papers?

They can. An AI answer may cite a preprint as though it were a journal article, describe an early finding as settled, or point to a preprint when a revised, published version says something different.

When an AI answer gives you a source, check three things: what kind of source it is, which version it points to and whether the paper says what the answer claims. The last check matters for every source, reviewed or not.

## Should patients act on findings from preprints?

Preprints can reach health news before any review. If a finding from a preprint seems relevant to your own care, bring it to your clinician rather than acting on it yourself. Research can be promising and still not be ready to guide decisions about a particular person.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are preprints peer reviewed?

No. A preprint is posted before peer review, and preprint servers generally mark them as not yet reviewed. Some are later reviewed and published by journals, sometimes with changes.

### Can I cite a preprint?

Yes, as long as you label it as a preprint. If the work has since been published in a journal, cite the published version instead.

### Do preprints get published later?

Some do and some do not. Search PubMed for the title and the authors' names to see whether a journal version exists, since titles can change during review.

### Is a preprint less reliable than a journal article?

A preprint has not had independent review, so its conclusions should be treated as provisional. Peer review does not guarantee a paper is right, but it adds a check that a preprint has not had yet.
