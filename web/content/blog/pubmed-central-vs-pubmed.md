PubMed Central is a free archive of full-text journal articles, while PubMed is a free search engine of citations and abstracts. Both are run by the National Library of Medicine at the National Institutes of Health, and a paper can have a record in PubMed without its full text being in PubMed Central.

Put simply, PubMed tells you a paper exists and what it is about. PubMed Central, when it has the paper, lets you read the whole thing.

## What is PubMed?

[PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) is a search engine for biomedical literature. Each record holds a citation (title, authors, journal and date), usually an abstract, and links to the full text where it is available. Many records also carry subject headings that describe the article, and every record has a PubMed ID (PMID), a number that identifies it.

PubMed does not host full text itself. It links out to full text on a publisher's website or in PubMed Central. For more on how it works, see [what PubMed is and who maintains it](/blog/what-is-pubmed) and [what a PMID is and how to look one up](/blog/what-is-a-pmid).

## What is PubMed Central?

[PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) is an archive of full-text articles from biomedical and life sciences journals. An article there is the complete paper: introduction, methods, results, discussion, figures, tables and reference list. Everything in it is free to read.

Each article in PubMed Central has its own identifier, written as the letters PMC followed by a number. A paper that is in both PubMed and PubMed Central has both a PMID and a PubMed Central identifier, and its PubMed record links to the full text.

Articles reach PubMed Central in a few ways. Some journals deposit their articles directly. Some papers arrive as author manuscripts, the version accepted after peer review, deposited to meet funder requirements such as those of the National Institutes of Health. An author manuscript can differ in formatting and copyediting from the final version in the journal, so note which one you are reading.

## How do PubMed and PubMed Central differ?

The two are easy to confuse because the names are close and the records link to each other. The differences:

- **What they hold.** PubMed holds citations and abstracts. PubMed Central holds full text.
- **Coverage.** Many papers with a PubMed record have no full text in PubMed Central. A missing full text does not mean the paper is less important, only that it has not been deposited there.
- **Identifiers.** PubMed uses the PMID. PubMed Central uses its own identifier, starting with the letters PMC.
- **What a search looks at.** A PubMed search looks at citations, abstracts and subject headings. A PubMed Central search looks through the full text, so it can find words that appear only in the methods or results.
- **Access.** Both are free to search. The full text you reach from a PubMed record may sit behind a paywall on a publisher's site. Full text in PubMed Central is free to read.

## How can you tell whether a paper has free full text?

A few quick checks:

1. **Look at the PubMed record.** PubMed marks records that have free full text and links to the full text in PubMed Central when it is there.
2. **Filter your search.** PubMed lets you limit results to records with free full text, which is useful when you need to read papers rather than only scan abstracts.
3. **Look for a PubMed Central identifier.** If the record shows one, the full text is in the archive.
4. **Check the publisher's page.** Some journals make articles free on their own sites, including papers that are not in PubMed Central.

## Is everything in PubMed Central free to reuse?

No. Free to read and free to reuse are different permissions, and copyright and license terms vary from article to article.

Some articles are published under licenses that allow broader reuse, while others keep the usual copyright restrictions. If you plan to republish text or figures, check the license or copyright statement on the article first.

## Why does the difference matter when you check a claim?

An abstract is a short summary written by the authors. It can leave out details of the methods, secondary results and the limitations the authors discuss in the body of the paper.

When a claim depends on one of those details, such as exactly who was studied or how an outcome was measured, the abstract may not settle it. You need the full text, and PubMed Central is the first free place to look. [Abstract versus full text](/blog/abstract-vs-full-text) explains when the abstract is not enough.

The same applies to claims in AI-generated answers. A general claim tied to a PMID can often be confirmed from the abstract. A claim about a specific subgroup, a secondary outcome or a detail of the methods usually needs the full paper, and a free full text in PubMed Central makes that check possible for anyone.

## How do you cite a paper you found in PubMed Central?

Cite the article itself, using the journal citation: authors, title, journal, year, volume and pages. Adding the PMID, the PubMed Central identifier or the digital object identifier (DOI) helps readers find it. If you read an author manuscript, the citation is still to the published article.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is PubMed Central the same as PubMed?

No. PubMed is a search engine of citations and abstracts, while PubMed Central is an archive of free full-text articles; both are run by the National Library of Medicine.

### Is everything in PubMed Central free?

Everything in PubMed Central is free to read, but not everything is free to reuse. Copyright and license terms vary by article, so check them before republishing text or figures.

### What is a PubMed Central ID?

A PubMed Central ID is the identifier for an article in PubMed Central, written as the letters PMC followed by a number. It is separate from the PubMed ID (PMID), and a paper in both databases has both.

### How do I find free full text for a paper in PubMed?

Look at the paper's PubMed record for a free full text link or a PubMed Central ID, or filter your search results to free full text. Some publishers also offer free full text on their own sites.
