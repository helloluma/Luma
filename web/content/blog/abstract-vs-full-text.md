An abstract is enough to decide whether a paper is relevant and to learn its main finding. It is not enough when your claim depends on details the abstract leaves out: who was studied, how outcomes were measured, what went wrong along the way, and how certain the authors really are.

Abstracts are short and usually free to read on PubMed, so they are the natural place to start. That makes them a sensible first step and a risky last one.

## What does an abstract actually contain?

An abstract is a short summary written by the authors. In clinical research, many abstracts follow a structured format with headings such as background, methods, results and conclusions. Its job is to help a reader decide whether to read the paper, and to make the paper findable in databases such as PubMed.

Because it is short, an abstract is a set of choices about what to keep. Authors usually keep the main question, the study design, the primary result and a conclusion. Much of the rest is cut.

For a walkthrough of each section, see [how to read a PubMed abstract in five minutes](/blog/how-to-read-a-pubmed-abstract).

## What do abstracts usually leave out?

These details often appear only in the full text:

- Inclusion and exclusion criteria. An abstract may say "adults," while the methods show that the study excluded people with certain other conditions.
- How outcomes were measured. A word like "improvement" can mean a lab value, a symptom score, or fewer hospital admissions.
- Secondary outcomes and subgroups. Results for specific groups may be reported only in tables.
- Harms and side effects. Safety data may be compressed into a single line or left out entirely.
- Dropouts and missing data. How many people left the study, and why, can change how much the result means.
- Limitations. Authors discuss weaknesses in the discussion section, which the abstract rarely has room to reflect.
- Funding and conflicts of interest. Disclosures usually sit at the end of the paper.
- The range of uncertainty. An abstract may report a main estimate without the confidence interval around it, or report one outcome while the others sit in the tables.

An abstract can also read more confidently than the paper behind it. A conclusion written for a summary has little space for the caveats that appear several pages later.

## When is the abstract enough?

For some tasks, the abstract does the job:

- Deciding whether a paper is relevant to your question.
- Confirming that a citation exists and that its title, authors, journal and year match.
- Getting the general direction of a finding before you read further.
- Screening a long list of search results.

## When do you need the full text?

Go to the full paper in these situations:

1. You are citing a specific number. Effect sizes, rates and follow-up periods should come from the results section and tables, where you can see exactly what they refer to.
2. Your claim is about a specific group. If you write that something holds for children or older adults, check that those people were in the study and that results were reported for them.
3. Your claim is about safety. Harms data may be summarized in a line or left out of the abstract.
4. The abstract is vague. Phrases like "may be associated with" or "showed promise" need the methods and results behind them.
5. You are describing the study design. Whether a study was randomized, blinded or controlled can be stated loosely in an abstract and precisely in the methods.
6. The claim carries weight. If a reader, a clinician or a product decision will rely on the sentence, read the source.

**If you would be uncomfortable defending the sentence to the paper's authors, read the paper.**

## How do you find the full text for free?

Start with the PubMed record. PubMed is a free search engine for biomedical literature maintained by the National Library of Medicine at the National Institutes of Health. It holds citations and abstracts, and it links out to full text where it is available. You can also filter PubMed search results to articles with free full text.

[PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) is a separate free full-text archive, also run by the National Library of Medicine. When a paper is in PubMed Central, its PubMed record links to it. For more on how the two differ, see [what PubMed Central is and how it differs from PubMed](/blog/pubmed-central-vs-pubmed).

Other routes to the full text:

- The journal's own website, since some articles are open access.
- Your institution's library, if you have access through one.
- The corresponding author, whose contact details are listed on the paper. You can write and ask for a copy.

## Why does this matter for AI-generated answers?

An AI tool that summarizes research from abstracts inherits every gap listed above. That creates a predictable failure: a claim that matches the abstract's headline but not the paper's details. The population in the answer is broader than the one studied, or a finding reported for one subgroup is stated as general.

This is one way [a real citation can still be the wrong citation](/blog/real-citation-wrong-claim). The paper exists, the abstract sounds close, and the specific claim is still not in it.

Automated checks help with the first pass. Luma, for example, searches PubMed for each claim in an AI answer and judges whether the retrieved evidence supports it. For a claim that carries weight, a person should still open the full paper.

## What should you check when you open the full text?

A short reading order that covers most claims:

1. Methods. Who was included and excluded, how outcomes were defined, and how long people were followed.
2. Results tables. The numbers behind your claim, including the subgroup or outcome you care about.
3. Limitations. Usually near the end of the discussion section.
4. Disclosures and funding. Usually at the end of the paper.
5. The conclusion. Compare the full-text conclusion with the abstract's version and note any difference in strength.

Then go back to your sentence. Check that the population, the exposure or intervention, the outcome and the strength of your wording all match what you just read. For a fuller checklist, see [how to tell whether a study actually supports a claim](/blog/how-to-tell-if-a-study-supports-a-claim).

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I cite a paper after reading only the abstract?

You can, but you take on the risk that the details do not match your sentence. For any specific number, subgroup or safety claim, read the full text before you cite it.

### Is PubMed a full-text database?

No. PubMed holds citations and abstracts, and it links to full text where available, including free articles in PubMed Central.

### How do I know if a paper has free full text?

Check the paper's PubMed record for a link to PubMed Central or to a free version on the publisher's site. PubMed also lets you filter search results to articles with free full text.

### Why might an abstract overstate a finding?

An abstract has little room for caveats, so limitations, subgroup restrictions and uncertainty described in the paper can be trimmed away. Comparing the abstract's conclusion with the paper's discussion section shows what was left out.
