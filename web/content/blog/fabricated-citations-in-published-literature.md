When a fabricated citation is published, the damage does not stay in one paper. The fake can be copied into later papers by authors who never checked it, it costs time for every reader who tries to follow it, and it weakens trust in reference lists generally, while the original paper usually stays uncorrected.

This is no longer a hypothetical problem. An audit by Topaz and colleagues, published in The Lancet in 2026, measured how often biomedical papers cite references that do not exist.

## What did the 2026 Lancet audit find?

Topaz and colleagues checked 97.1 million structured references across about 2.5 million biomedical papers. They found 4,046 fabricated references across 2,810 papers.

The rate has climbed. Roughly one in 277 papers published in the first seven weeks of 2026 cited a paper that does not exist, up from one in 458 in 2025 and one in 2,828 in 2023.

The audit also reported that prior studies estimate 30 to 69 percent of references generated by large language models in biomedical contexts are fabricated. And **98.4 percent of affected papers had received no publisher action at the time of the audit.**

For a closer look at the audit's numbers, see [how common fabricated references are in biomedical papers](/blog/how-common-are-fabricated-references-biomedical-papers).

## How does a fake reference spread?

A fabricated reference in a published paper looks like any other reference. It has authors, a title, a journal and a year. Once it sits in a peer-reviewed article, it borrows that article's credibility.

The spread happens through copying. When an author takes a reference from another paper's reference list without opening the source, any error in that reference travels with it. The next reader now sees two published papers that appear to point to the same source, which makes the nonexistent paper look more established.

Search tools and AI systems read published text too. A fake reference sitting in a published paper can be retrieved and repeated by any tool that treats published text as reliable by default.

## Who pays the cost?

The harm is spread thinly across many people, which makes it easy to overlook:

- Readers lose time hunting for a paper that does not exist, and may assume they searched badly.
- Librarians and information specialists field requests for sources that cannot be found.
- Researchers writing reviews run into dead ends when they try to trace the evidence behind a claim.
- Peer reviewers and editors carry the work of catching fakes before publication.
- Real researchers whose names appear on fake references have work attributed to them that they never wrote.

The last group is easy to forget. A fabricated reference can carry plausible author names, and those names may belong to real people working in the field.

## Why does one fake citation undermine the paper around it?

A citation is a promise that the sentence has evidence behind it. When the cited source does not exist, the sentence has no support from that reference, whether or not the sentence happens to be true.

The reader then faces a fair question about the rest of the reference list. If one source was never checked, which others were? A single fabricated reference can cast doubt on claims in the paper that were properly sourced.

In medicine, the effect reaches beyond the paper. Clinical summaries, patient materials and guidelines draw on the published literature. A claim that looks supported because it carries a citation can be given more weight than it deserves.

## What happens to papers after the fakes are found?

Corrections and retractions are the formal tools the literature has for fixing the record. PubMed shows retraction notices on retracted records, so a reader who finds a retracted paper there is warned.

The audit's finding on publisher action matters here. With 98.4 percent of affected papers having received no publisher action at the time of the audit, a reader who opens one of those papers finds no correction notice warning that anything is wrong. The fake references stay in circulation, where later authors can copy them.

## What can researchers do?

1. Look up every reference before you cite it, including ones you copied from another paper's list. Search the PubMed ID (PMID), the digital object identifier (DOI), or the exact title, and confirm the authors, journal and year match.
2. Read at least the abstract to confirm the source supports your sentence.
3. Never cite a reference supplied by an AI tool without finding the paper yourself.
4. Report what you find. If you discover a fabricated reference in a published paper, tell the journal.
5. Keep a record of how you checked each reference, so a co-author or reviewer can repeat the check.

Step-by-step guidance is in [how to check if a PubMed citation is real](/blog/how-to-check-if-a-pubmed-citation-is-real) and [how to verify an AI-generated reference list](/blog/verify-ai-generated-reference-list).

## What can editors and journals do?

- Check at submission that each reference identifier resolves to the paper it names.
- Ask authors to confirm that they have read every source they cite.
- Treat a fabricated reference as a reason to look more closely at the rest of the manuscript.
- Act on reports of fabricated references in published papers with a correction or other notice, so the record carries a warning.

Practical screening steps for editorial teams are covered in [how journal editors and peer reviewers can screen for fabricated references](/blog/editors-reviewers-screen-fabricated-references).

Automated checks can take the first pass at scale. Luma, for example, checks each claim in a text against PubMed, links the claims the evidence supports to their sources, and flags what it cannot verify. A person still makes the final call.
