To prepare for a doctor's visit, write down your symptoms and questions, bring a complete list of your medications, and plan to ask about next steps before you leave. AI tools can help you organize those notes and understand unfamiliar terms, but they cannot examine you, diagnose you or replace what your clinician tells you.

## What should you write down before a doctor's visit?

Appointments move quickly, and it is easy to forget the one thing you came in to ask. A short written list solves most of that.

For each symptom or concern, note:

- What it is, described in your own words
- When it started, and whether it comes and goes
- What makes it better or worse
- What you have already tried
- How it affects your daily life, your work or your sleep

Then write your questions, with the most important one first. If time runs short, the top of the list is what gets answered.

## What should you bring to the appointment?

- A list of every medication you take, with the name and strength as printed on the label, plus any over-the-counter medicines, vitamins and supplements
- Any allergies, and any reactions you have had to medicines in the past
- Results, records or letters from other clinicians, especially ones outside the same health system
- Your insurance card and identification, if the office asks for them
- Your symptom notes and your list of questions
- Something to take notes with

Bringing a family member or friend can help. A second person may remember details you miss and can ask a question you forget. If you would like to record the conversation, ask your clinician first. There are more ideas in [how to keep track of what your doctor said](/blog/how-to-keep-track-of-what-your-doctor-said).

## What questions should you ask during the visit?

Your own list comes first. These general questions fit almost any appointment:

- What do you think is going on, and what else could it be?
- What are the next steps, and why are they the right ones for me?
- If you are ordering a test, what is it for, and how and when will I get the results?
- What changes should I watch for, and when should I contact you or seek care sooner?
- Is there a reliable place where I can read more about this?

If a test is part of the plan, the article on [questions to ask about your test results](/blog/questions-to-ask-about-your-test-results) covers what to ask when the results come back.

Before you leave, **repeat the plan back in your own words**. If something you say does not match what your clinician meant, they can correct it on the spot.

## How can AI help you prepare?

AI chat tools can be useful for the organizing part of preparation. They can help with:

- Turning scattered notes into a clear list of symptoms with dates
- Suggesting questions you may not have thought to ask
- Explaining a medical term from an earlier visit summary or letter in plain language
- Putting your concerns in order so the most important ones come first
- Drafting a short summary of your history to bring with you

Treat what the tool produces as a draft. Read it, correct it, and cut anything that does not match your own experience. The article on [what patients should know before asking AI about their health](/blog/what-patients-should-know-before-asking-ai-about-health) goes further into using these tools carefully.

## What can't AI do for your health?

An AI tool cannot examine you, see your full records or weigh your history the way your clinician can. It does not know which details matter most in your case, and it can be wrong while sounding completely sure of itself.

Keep these limits in mind:

- It cannot diagnose you. A list of possible conditions from a chatbot is not a diagnosis, and it can cause needless worry or false reassurance.
- It can get facts and sources wrong. A statistic or a reference that looks real may not exist, a problem explained in [what an AI hallucination in medicine is](/blog/what-is-an-ai-hallucination-in-medicine).
- It should not guide treatment decisions. Do not start, stop or change a medicine because of an AI answer. Talk to your clinician first.
- It may not keep your information private. Before typing in personal health details, check what the tool does with them, and share only what you need to.

If something an AI tool told you is worrying you, bring it to the visit and ask about it directly. Your clinician can tell you whether it applies to you at all.

## What should you do after the visit?

Read over your notes the same day, while the conversation is fresh. If your clinic offers a patient portal or a written visit summary, compare it with your notes and mark anything that does not match.

If something is still unclear, contact the office and ask. A short question now is easier than guessing later. Keep your notes together with any results that come back, so you have the full picture ready for your next appointment.

## Frequently asked questions

### What should I bring to a doctor's appointment?

Bring a list of all your medications and supplements, any allergies, records or results from other clinicians, your insurance information, and a written list of your symptoms and questions.

### How do I make sure my questions get answered at a doctor's visit?

Write your questions down before the visit and put the most important ones first. Mention near the start of the appointment that you have a few questions, so there is time to cover them.

### Can AI diagnose my symptoms?

No. AI tools can help you organize notes and understand terms, but they cannot examine you or see your full history, and they can be wrong while sounding confident. Only your clinician can diagnose you.

### Is it okay to use AI to prepare questions for my doctor?

Yes, as a starting point. Use it to organize your thoughts and suggest questions, then review the list yourself and bring it to your clinician, who can tell you what applies to you.
