A conflict of interest disclosure lists the financial and personal ties that could bear on a study, and in most papers you will find it near the end of the full text, next to the funding statement. Read it as a map of where to look harder: a declared conflict does not make a study wrong, and "none declared" does not prove a study is free of influence.

## What is a conflict of interest in medical research?

A conflict of interest exists when an author, or the organization paying for the work, has an interest that could influence how a study is designed, run, analyzed or reported. The key word is "could". A disclosure describes a risk, and whether bias actually shaped the study is a separate question you answer by reading the study itself.

Common kinds of conflict include:

- Funding for the study from a company that makes the drug, device or test being studied.
- Personal payments to an author, such as consulting fees, speaking fees or advisory board roles.
- Ownership interests, such as stock, patents or royalties tied to the product.
- Employment by a company with a stake in the result.
- Non-financial ties, such as a strong public position on the question, a professional rivalry or a personal relationship.

## Where do conflict of interest disclosures appear in a paper?

In most journal articles, the disclosure sits near the end of the paper, after the main text and before the reference list. Look for a heading such as competing interests, conflicts of interest, declarations or disclosures. A separate funding statement says who paid for the work, and many papers add a sentence about what role, if any, the funder played.

Placement varies by journal. If you do not see a disclosure at the end, check the footnotes on the first page, the acknowledgments and any supplementary files.

The abstract usually leaves all of this out. If you have only read an abstract, you have probably not seen who funded the study or what ties the authors reported. Our guide to [when the abstract is not enough](/blog/abstract-vs-full-text) explains how to get to the full paper, including free full text in [PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) when it is available.

## How do you read a disclosure statement?

Work through it in this order:

1. Identify who has the tie. Disclosures are usually listed author by author, often by initials. A tie held by the lead or senior author deserves more attention than one held by a co-author with a small role.
2. Match the tie to the topic. A payment from the company whose product is under study matters more than a tie to a company with no stake in the question.
3. Read the funding statement. Note who paid, and whether the money was for this study or for general support.
4. Read the statement on the funder's role. Many papers say whether the funder took part in designing the study, collecting or analyzing the data, writing the paper or deciding to publish. When a funder's employees did any of these, the paper should say so.
5. Treat "none declared" as the authors' own report. Disclosure depends on what authors tell the journal, so it is a statement from them rather than an independent check.

## Why should a conflict make you look closer instead of dismissing the study?

Companies often pay for research on their own products, because they are the ones developing them and seeking approval to sell them. If every study with industry funding were thrown out, you would lose evidence that may be perfectly sound, including some of the trials that showed a treatment works.

**A conflict tells you which way the incentives point.** It does not tell you whether those incentives changed anything. The useful response is to look at the choices in the study where influence, if there was any, would show up.

## What should you check more closely when a conflict is declared?

- The comparison. Note whether the product was tested against a placebo, against no treatment, or against the best available alternative. A comparison with a weak alternative can make a product look better than it would against standard care.
- The outcomes. Note whether the main outcome is something patients feel or experience, such as symptoms or survival, or a lab marker that may not translate into a health benefit.
- Whether the outcomes were set in advance. Many trials are registered in a public registry before they begin. If the primary outcome in the paper differs from the registered one, the paper should explain why.
- Size and duration. A small or short study may miss side effects that take longer, or more patients, to appear.
- The conclusion's wording. Compare what the conclusion claims with what the results show. Our checklist on [how to tell whether a study actually supports a claim](/blog/how-to-tell-if-a-study-supports-a-claim) covers this step in detail.
- Independent work. Look for studies with different funders that tested the same question. A finding repeated by groups with no shared interest is harder to explain away, which is one reason [one study is rarely the final word](/blog/why-one-study-is-rarely-the-final-word).

## How do conflicts of interest affect AI answers that cite research?

AI answers and summaries usually report a finding and its citation, and leave out the disclosure. Even a citation that is real and supports the sentence tells you nothing about who paid for the study or what ties its authors had.

Claim-checking tools, Luma included, answer a narrower question: does published research support this claim, and which paper says so. Reading that paper's disclosure is still your job. If a study bears on your own health or care, bring it to your clinician and ask how it fits with the rest of the evidence.

## Frequently asked questions

### Where is the conflict of interest statement in a research paper?

In most papers it is near the end of the full text, just before the reference list, under a heading such as competing interests, conflicts of interest or disclosures. If it is not there, check the first-page footnotes, the acknowledgments and any supplementary files.

### Does industry funding mean a study is biased?

Not by itself. Funding from a company with a stake in the result is a reason to examine the study's comparison, outcomes, size and conclusions more closely, and to look for independent studies of the same question.

### What does "none declared" mean in a disclosure?

It means the authors reported no conflicts of interest. Disclosure depends on what authors report, so "none declared" is their own statement rather than an independent verification.

### Do non-financial conflicts of interest count?

Yes. A strong public position, a professional rivalry or a personal relationship can also shape how research is designed or interpreted, and some journals ask authors to declare these ties too.
