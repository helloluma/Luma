An AI hallucination in medicine is a statement an AI tool presents as fact that is false, unsupported by evidence, or tied to a source that does not exist or does not say it. The term covers invented facts, invented references and real references attached to claims they do not support, and all of them read just as confidently as correct output.

Knowing the types makes them easier to catch.

## Why is it called a hallucination?

The word describes output that has no basis in reality but is presented as real. A language model generates text by predicting what plausibly comes next, based on patterns in the material it was trained on. It has no built-in step where it looks a fact up and confirms it before writing.

When the pattern produces something plausible but wrong, the result is a hallucination. Other words for the same thing include fabrication and confabulation. The label matters less than the behavior: the output reads as fact, and it is not supported by any real source.

## What types of AI hallucination show up in health information?

### Invented facts

The model states something false. It may give the wrong mechanism for a drug, mix up two conditions with similar names, or describe a side effect that belongs to a different medicine. ACE inhibitors are known to cause a dry cough in some people, for example. A hallucinated answer could attach that side effect to an unrelated drug and state it with full confidence.

### Invented sources

The model produces a reference to a paper that was never published. A fabricated reference can have realistic author names, a real journal, a plausible year and a title that sounds exactly right.

An audit by Topaz and colleagues, published in The Lancet in 2026, reports that prior studies estimate 30 to 69 percent of references generated by large language models in biomedical contexts are fabricated. We explain the mechanism in [what a fabricated citation is](/blog/what-is-a-fabricated-citation).

### Misattributed sources

The paper is real, but it does not say what the sentence claims. The study may have looked at a different population, measured a different outcome, or reported a preliminary result that the answer states as settled.

Misattribution is harder to catch than invention, because a quick check confirms the paper exists. See [why a real citation can still be the wrong citation](/blog/real-citation-wrong-claim) for how it happens.

### Overstated certainty

A cautious finding becomes a firm rule. "Was associated with" turns into "causes." The phrase "in a small trial" disappears. Each change is minor on its own, but together they turn a tentative result into what looks like established medical fact.

## Why are hallucinations dangerous in medicine?

Health information gets acted on. A reader may change a habit, worry about a symptom, or bring a wrong idea into a conversation with a clinician. A professional may copy an AI-drafted paragraph and its references into a report, a patient handout or a paper.

Fabricated references are already in the published record. The same audit by Topaz and colleagues found 4,046 fabricated references across 2,810 papers in the biomedical literature, and found that 98.4 percent of affected papers had received no publisher action at the time of the audit. Once a fake reference is in print, other authors can copy it into new work.

The other problem is that hallucinations look the same as facts. AI output is uniformly polished, so a wrong sentence carries the same tone as a right one. Our post on [why fluent writing makes AI medical errors harder to spot](/blog/why-fluent-ai-writing-hides-errors) looks at why readers miss them.

## How do you catch an AI hallucination?

You can catch most of the types above with a methodical check.

1. Break the answer into individual claims. A mostly correct paragraph can hide one wrong sentence.
2. For each reference, confirm it exists. Look up the PubMed ID (PMID) or the exact title on PubMed, and check that the authors, journal and year match the citation.
3. Read the abstract against the sentence it is meant to support. Look for the same population, the same outcome and the same level of certainty.
4. For claims with no reference, search for evidence yourself or treat the claim as unverified.
5. Take extra care with specific details: numbers, drug names, study names and quotations.

## Can AI hallucinations be prevented?

They can be reduced. Systems that retrieve real documents before answering give the model actual text to work from, which makes invented references less likely. Design choices help too. A system that is allowed to say "I could not find support for this" has less reason to guess.

Reducing hallucinations is different from removing them. A model working from real documents can still misread them, blend them, or cite the wrong one for a given sentence. **Checking each claim against its source is still the step that catches what slips through.**

## Frequently asked questions

### What is an example of an AI hallucination in medicine?

One example is a reference to a journal article that does not exist, complete with plausible authors, a real journal and a believable year. Another is a real study cited for a finding it never reported.

### Why do AI models hallucinate?

Language models generate text by predicting plausible next words from patterns in their training data. They do not look facts up as they write, so they can produce fluent statements and references that have no basis in any real source.

### How can I tell if an AI medical answer contains a hallucination?

Check each specific claim and each reference. Confirm that cited papers exist on PubMed and that their abstracts actually support the sentences they are attached to.

### Are AI hallucinations only about fake citations?

No. They also include invented facts, real sources cited for claims they do not support, and cautious findings restated as certain.
